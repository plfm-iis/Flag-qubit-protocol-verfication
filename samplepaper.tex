\section{Introduction}

Quantum error correction is essential for realizing reliable quantum
computation on noisy physical devices~\cite{Shor96}. Quantum error-correcting
codes (QECCs) encode logical information into multiple physical qubits and
provide protection against physical faults~\cite{CS96,Ste96}. 
% For a code ofdistance $d$, ideal error correction can correct any Pauli error of weight at most $t=\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor$. 
In practice, however, the error-correction
circuitry is itself imperfect, and faults during syndrome extraction can
propagate into high-weight data errors and cause logical failures.


Fault-tolerant error correction (FTEC) addresses this problem by designing
syndrome extraction and recovery procedures that remain reliable in the
presence of circuit faults~\cite{Shor96,AGP06,CDT09:CCS:2011814.2011815}.
Early approaches by Shor~\cite{Shor96}, Steane~\cite{Steane97}, and Knill~\cite{Knill05}
use carefully prepared ancillary states and verification circuits, but can
incur substantial resource overhead.
Flagged fault-tolerant error correction
provides an alternative based on a small number of additional ancilla qubits,
called \emph{flag qubits}~\cite{CR18a,CR18b}, and has since been extended to
higher-distance codes and improved through parallelization, flag sharing, and
adaptive low-depth control flow~\cite{CB18,reichardt2021fault,LL23,Liou25,Du24,low-depth}.



% \emph{Flagged fault-tolerant error correction protocols}~\cite{CR18a,CR18b} provide an alternative approach to fault-tolerant error correction using a small number of additional qubits. Instead of using large verified ancilla states, these protocols introduce simple ancilla qubits, called \emph{flag qubits}, into syndrome extraction circuits. When an internal fault may propagate to a high-weight data error, the flag measurement outcome provides  information that enables the \emph{decoder} to select an appropriate recovery operation. The original flagged scheme was introduced by Chao and Reichardt~\cite{CR18a} for distance-3 codes and was later extended to higher-distance stabilizer codes by Chamberland and Beverland~\cite{CB18}. Subsequent work has further improved flagged protocols through parallelization, flag sharing, and adaptive low-depth control flow~\cite{reichardt2021fault,LL23,Liou25,Du24,low-depth}.
% Despite their resource advantages, flagged protocols are challenging to design and verify. 
% A flagged fault-tolerant error correction protocol must ensure that any two correctable fault events are either distinguishable from the measurement outcomes or produce equivalent residual errors on the data qubits.
% Otherwise, the decoder may fail to identify a recovery operation that correctly handles both cases. Establishing this property requires careful coordination of gate ordering, qubit reuse, measurement scheduling, classical control flow, and the decoding procedure.

Despite their resource advantages, flagged protocols are challenging to
design and verify. Their performance depends on the detailed structure of
the syndrome extraction circuits and the adaptive control flow, including
gate ordering, qubit reuse, measurement scheduling, and the choice of
subsequent syndrome extraction circuits based on previous measurement
outcomes. Different fault events may produce the same measurement record,
so the measurement record may not contain sufficient information to determine
a valid recovery.

In this paper, we develop a systematic framework for the verification of
flagged fault-tolerant error correction protocols. We focus on whether the
information generated by a protocol is sufficient to support a fault-tolerant
recovery. Here, a \emph{measurement record} consists of the syndrome and flag
outcomes collected along an adaptive execution path. For a fixed terminating
path and measurement record, multiple residual Pauli errors may be compatible
with the observed outcomes. The central question is whether a single recovery
can correctly handle all such residual errors.


% % A flagged protocol must be checked not only for Pauli fault propagation
% % through its syndrome extraction circuits, but also for whether its
% % \emph{measurement record} contains sufficient information for fault-tolerant
% % recovery. For a fixed terminating path and measurement record, multiple
% % residual Pauli errors may be compatible with the observed outcomes. The key
% % question is whether these residual errors can be handled by a common recovery.

% % This observation exposes the central verification problem addressed in this paper. 
% A flagged protocol must be checked not only for Pauli fault propagation
% through its syndrome extraction circuits, but also for whether its \emph{measurement
% record} contains sufficient information for fault-tolerant recovery. 
% % Themeasurement record consists of the \emph{syndrome and flag outcomes} collected along an adaptive execution path. 


Existing formal verification methods reason about recovery using
assertions on decoder outputs~\cite{chen2025verifying} or conditions
derived for the decoder~\cite{10.1145/3729293}.
These methods can avoid executing a concrete decoder implementation.
However, failure to verify a protocol under a particular decoder
or decoder specification does not establish that the protocol
cannot support fault-tolerant recovery: another decoder may succeed.
We therefore formulate the verification condition directly in terms
of the residual-error sets associated with measurement records,
allowing us to check whether the protocol admits a suitable decoder
without selecting one in advance.



%A decoder-dependent verification approach would first construct a lookup-table decoder and then check whether the resulting correction satisfies the fault-tolerant error-correction conditions for every fault event within the fault bound. This is not the most informative verification target. If such a check fails, the failure may arise either from the choice of decoder or from the protocol itself: the syndrome and flag outcomes may simply fail to distinguish fault events that require different recoveries. We therefore formulate the verification condition directly in terms of the residual-error sets associated with measurement records, rather than a preselected decoder.


Our main characterization shows that, when the syndrome extraction circuits
and the flagged error correction protocol are valid, an FTEC lookup-table
decoder exists if and only if no two residual errors associated with the
same measurement record differ by a
\emph{nontrivial logical operator}~\cite{KL97,Got97}.
When this condition holds, each measurement record can be assigned a recovery that corrects
all compatible residual errors. When it fails, the same measurement record is
compatible with two logically inequivalent residual errors, and no recovery
depending only on that record can correctly handle both cases.


Based on this characterization, we develop a verification framework.
We first verify circuit validity, i.e., the \emph{correctness} and \emph{determinism} of the syndrome extraction
circuits. Only if circuit validity passes do we check \emph{error-set correctability}
and \emph{protocol validity}, which includes \emph{path conditions} %control-flow termination \yfc{we did not check termination}
and \emph{flag requirements}. If all checks pass, the protocol admits a decoder satisfying \emph{weak FTEC}.
Our verification combines symbolic execution, SAT solving, and
stabilizer simulation. We symbolically execute protocol paths,
tracking Pauli errors and measurement outcomes as Boolean formulas
over fault variables, and encode violations of correctability,
path conditions, and flag requirements as SAT queries.
Circuit validity is checked using symbolic Pauli propagation
and signed stabilizer simulation.

%We symbolically execute protocol paths, tracking Pauli errors and measurement outcomes as Boolean formulas over fault variables, and use SAT queries to search for incompatible residual errors with the same measurement record. A satisfying assignment gives a correctability counterexample; when all such queries are unsatisfiable and protocol validity holds, our characterization establishes the existence of an FTEC lookup-table decoder.



We implement the method and evaluate it on representative flagged protocols from the literature. 
The evaluation validates known constructions
by proving that their concrete formal instantiations satisfy the required
validity and FTEC conditions, and exposes errors that are difficult
to detect by manual inspection.
Translating published protocol descriptions into formal models or
implementations is itself error-prone, as correctness depends on subtle
details of gate ordering, measurement conventions, and control flow.
Our verifier supports this process by producing counterexamples that
guide model refinement and help distinguish modeling errors from flaws
in the underlying protocol.
%The evaluation demonstrates two uses of the verifier. First, it validates known constructions by proving that their concrete formal instantiations satisfy the required validity and FTEC conditions. Second, it exposes errors that are difficult to detect by manual inspection. During benchmark construction, the verifier repeatedly caught concrete instantiation mistakes while translating protocol descriptions into executable formal models. More substantively, it
It also identified previously unknown design flaws in published protocols, including counterexamples in low-depth protocols for the
$[[5,1,3]]$ and $[[7,1,3]]$ codes and flag-condition violations in
higher-distance protocols. Some findings were confirmed through communication with the original authors. Together, these results show that automated verification
is useful not only for checking new protocol designs, but also for ensuring that
published protocols are faithfully instantiated and for detecting subtle
correctness bugs in the protocols themselves.





\section{Preliminaries}
This section introduces the notation and conventions used throughout the paper. 
We first review the binary representation of Pauli operators and the fundamental notions of stabilizer codes, including syndromes, degeneracy, and logical equivalence.
%In particular, we establish a formal criterion for verifying whether a syndrome extraction circuit correctly implements the intended stabilizer measurements. The criterion requires that, after measurement, the measurement qubits are disentangled from the data block, and that the fault-free circuit produces measurement outcomes consistent with the eigenvalues of the measured stabilizers.
 Finally, we define the symbolic Pauli propagation semantics and the circuit-level fault model used by our verifier.



\subsection{Pauli operators and binary representation}
\label{subsec:pauli-binary}

We represent Pauli errors modulo global phases in $\{\pm1,\pm i\}$,
which do not affect error propagation, syndromes, or logical equivalence.
The signs of measured Pauli observables, however, are retained when
interpreting measurement outcomes.
We use the standard binary representation~\cite{NC00}:
\begin{equation}
I\mapsto(0,0),\quad X\mapsto(1,0),\quad
Z\mapsto(0,1),\quad Y\mapsto(1,1).
\end{equation}

An $n$-qubit \emph{Pauli string}
$P=P_1\otimes\cdots\otimes P_n$ is represented by
\begin{equation}
P=(P_{1,x},P_{1,z},\ldots,P_{n,x},P_{n,z}),
\label{eq:binary}
\end{equation}
where $(P_{i,x},P_{i,z})$ denotes the binary representation
of $P_i$. We use the same notation for a Pauli operator
and its binary representation when clear from context.
Pauli multiplication, denoted by $\cdot$, corresponds
to bitwise XOR modulo global phase:
\begin{equation}
P\cdot Q=P\oplus Q.
\label{eq:pauli_prod}
\end{equation}
The \emph{weight} $\mathrm{wt}(P)$ is the number of
non-identity components of $P$.

The binary symplectic product determines whether Pauli strings
$P$ and $Q$ commute:
\begin{equation}
P\odot Q=
\bigoplus_{j=1}^{n}
\left(
(P_{j,x}\land Q_{j,z})
\oplus
(P_{j,z}\land Q_{j,x})
\right).
\label{eq:symp_ip}
\end{equation}
Here, $\oplus$ and $\land$ denote Boolean XOR and AND.
The operators commute if $P\odot Q=0$ and
anticommute otherwise; equivalently,
$P\cdot Q=(-1)^{P\odot Q}Q\cdot P$.


\subsection{Stabilizer codes, syndromes, and degeneracy}
\label{subsec:stablizer-syndromes-degeneracy}

An $[[n,k,d]]$ \emph{stabilizer code} encodes $k$ logical
qubits into $n$ physical qubits with distance $d$~\cite{Got97}.
Its $n-k$ independent, mutually commuting Pauli generators
$G_1,\ldots,G_{n-k}$ generate the stabilizer group
$\mathcal{S}=\langle G_1,\ldots,G_{n-k}\rangle$.
The code space is their common $+1$ eigenspace.
The logical Pauli operators
$\mathcal{L}=\{\bar X_1,\ldots,\bar X_k,
\bar Z_1,\ldots,\bar Z_k\}$
generate the logical Pauli group modulo $\mathcal{S}$.

Two Pauli errors $E_1$ and $E_2$ are
\emph{stabilizer-equivalent}, written $E_1\sim E_2$,
if $E_1\cdot E_2\in\mathcal{S}$ up to global phase.
Such errors act identically on encoded states.
The minimum weight of an error up to stabilizers is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{wt}_{\min}(P)
=\min_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\mathrm{wt}(P\cdot S).
\label{eq:wt_min}
\end{equation}

The \emph{syndrome} of a Pauli error $E$ is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{syn}(E)
=(E\odot G_1,\ldots,E\odot G_{n-k}),
\label{eq:syndrome}
\end{equation}
where $\odot$ is the binary symplectic product.
The syndrome map is linear:
$\mathrm{syn}(E\cdot F)
=\mathrm{syn}(E)\oplus\mathrm{syn}(F)$.

The \emph{normalizer} $N(\mathcal{S})$ consists of
Pauli operators commuting with every stabilizer.
Its elements have zero syndrome, while
$N(\mathcal{S})\setminus\mathcal{S}$ consists of
nontrivial logical Pauli operators.
The code distance $d$ is the minimum weight of
an operator in $N(\mathcal{S})\setminus\mathcal{S}$.
A set $\mathcal{E}$ of Pauli errors is
\emph{correctable} if and only if~\cite{KL97,Got97}
\begin{equation}
\forall E_1,E_2\in\mathcal{E},\quad
E_1^\dagger\cdot E_2
\notin N(\mathcal{S})\setminus\mathcal{S}.
\label{eq:QECC_condition}
\end{equation}
Here, $E^\dagger=E$ up to global phase.

Consequently, any two errors of weight at most
$t=\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor$ with the same syndrome
are stabilizer-equivalent.
An \emph{ideal decoder} performs fault-free syndrome
extraction and applies a corresponding correction,
thereby correcting any error with
$\mathrm{wt}_{\min}(E)\le t$.





\subsection{Symbolic Pauli propagation}

We symbolically track Pauli errors using a pair of Boolean
formulas $(Q_{i,x},Q_{i,z})$ per qubit $i$, avoiding
fault enumeration and quantum-state simulation.

We consider Clifford circuits with ancillas initialized
in $\ket{0}$ or $\ket{+}$ and measured in the $X$ or $Z$
basis. Since Clifford gates preserve Pauli operators
under conjugation, error propagation can be represented
by Boolean updates. Figure~\ref{fig:two-qubit-pauli-rules}
shows the CX and CZ propagation rules used by our verifier.
For a qubit with symbolic error $(Q_{i,x},Q_{i,z})$,
a $Z$-basis measurement yields the error-induced outcome
bit $Q_{i,x}$, while an $X$-basis measurement yields
$Q_{i,z}$.

\subsection{Circuit-level noise model}
\label{sec:circuit-level-noise-model}

We use a circuit-level Pauli fault model, in which a
\emph{fault} at a circuit location is represented by a
Pauli error inserted after the corresponding ideal
operation. For a single-qubit location, a fault applies
a non-identity Pauli operator from $\{X,Y,Z\}$. For a
two-qubit location acting on qubits $i$ and $j$, a fault
applies a nontrivial two-qubit Pauli error
$P_i\otimes P_j$, where $P_i,P_j\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}$ and
$(P_i,P_j)\neq (I,I)$.

Preparation and measurement faults introduce Pauli errors immediately after state preparation and just before measurement, respectively. Meanwhile, idle faults apply a non-identity single-qubit Pauli error to any inactive qubit, and gate faults insert Pauli errors on the specific qubits acted upon by the gate.

\section{Fault-tolerant error correction and flagged syndrome extraction}\label{sec:flagged-SE}
This section introduces our formal framework for flagged error correction protocols and FTEC. We start by formalizing basic unflagged syndrome extraction circuits, which we then extend to define the full flagged error correction protocol, including its underlying circuits and classical control flow. After formally defining protocol validity, we establish the necessary and sufficient conditions for a valid protocol to achieve FTEC.



\subsection{Syndrome extraction circuits}
\label{subsec:syndrome extraction-protocols}

A \emph{syndrome extraction circuit} couples data qubits
to ancillas initialized in $\ket{0}$ or $\ket{+}$ and
measures them to extract stabilizer syndromes.
Multiple extraction circuits may execute in parallel,
provided their correctness and measurement determinism
are preserved.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:syndrome-extraction-correct}
A Clifford syndrome extraction circuit $U$ for Pauli
checks $\{P_i\}_{i=1}^{m}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{data}}$
is \emph{correct} if, for every input data Pauli error $E$,
the propagated error
\[
U(E\otimes I_{\mathrm{anc}})U^\dagger
=\omega(E'\otimes E_{\mathrm{anc}})
\]
satisfies
\[
E_{\mathrm{anc}}\odot A_i=E\odot P_i
\quad(i=1,\ldots,m),\qquad E'\sim E,
\]
where $A_i$ is the designated ancilla measurement
observable and $\omega$ is a global Pauli phase.
\end{definition}

Correctness of a syndrome-extraction circuit ensures the intended syndrome parities and preserves
data errors up to stabilizer equivalence, but does not ensure
measurement determinism.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:syndrome-extraction-deterministic}
A syndrome extraction circuit is \emph{deterministic}
if, in the absence of circuit faults, every input
data Pauli error determines all ancilla measurement
outcomes independently of the encoded state, and
the output data remains in the codespace up to
a Pauli error. For an error-free input, all ancilla
outcomes are $+1$, and the encoded logical state
is preserved up to global phase.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
\label{def:syndrome-extraction-valid}
A circuit is \emph{valid}
if correct
(Def.~\ref{def:syndrome-extraction-correct})
and deterministic
(Def.~\ref{def:syndrome-extraction-deterministic}).
\end{definition}

The following theorem characterizes validity using
stabilizer and logical-operator preservation, enabling
an efficient check via stabilizer simulation.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:syndrome-extraction-valid}
Let $U$ be a Clifford syndrome extraction circuit
before ancilla measurements, with
\[
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{joint}}
=\langle G_1,\ldots,G_{n-k},A_1,\ldots,A_r\rangle,
\]
where $G_i$ are data stabilizer generators and
$A_i$ are the single-qubit ancilla preparation
and measurement observables. Among the $r$ ancillas,
the first $m$ extract the designated Pauli checks
$\{P_i\}_{i=1}^{m}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{data}}$.
Let $L_1,\ldots,L_{2k}$ be a complete set of logical
Pauli generators, extended by identities on the ancillas.

Then $U$ is valid if and only if it is correct and
\[
\begin{gathered}
U\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{joint}}U^\dagger
=\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{joint}},\\
(UL_iU^\dagger)L_i\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{joint}}
\qquad(i=1,\ldots,2k).
\end{gathered}
\]
Both stabilizer equality and membership retain
Pauli signs.
\end{theorem}

The proof is given in Appendix~\ref{app:proof-syndrome-extraction}.
Figure~\ref{fig:three-part-syndrome-bad} illustrates
an invalid parallel syndrome extraction circuit
from~\cite{KL24}. Although the individual circuits
extract $XX$ and $ZZ$ correctly, their parallel
execution entangles the ancillas, making the
measurement outcomes nondeterministic.
Thus, circuit validity cannot be inferred from
the validity of individual extraction circuits.

The \emph{ideal syndrome} of a data error $E$
with respect to a Pauli check $P$ is $E\odot P$,
whereas the \emph{measured syndrome} consists of
the actual ancilla measurement outcomes.
A \emph{full syndrome extraction round} measures
a generating set of data stabilizers; if valid and fault-free,
it reports $\mathrm{syn}(E)$.

\subsection{Equivalent circuit-level noise model}

For verification, we consider the possible Pauli errors induced by circuit faults, rather than their probabilities. The following reduction covers bounded-fault executions using only two-qubit gate fault locations. Input data errors are counted separately from circuit faults, as in Section~\ref{sec:circuit-level-noise-model}. 
In the syndrome-extraction circuits considered, every single-qubit fault location is immediately adjacent along its wire to a two-qubit gate. A single-qubit Pauli fault therefore has a preceding or following two-qubit gate on the same qubit to which it can be moved.



\begin{proposition}
\label{prop}
For the Clifford syndrome extraction circuits considered here, it is sufficient for bounded-fault verification to introduce fault variables only at two-qubit gate locations. Every execution with at most $t$ circuit faults, including faults at state preparation, measurement, idle, and single-qubit gate locations, has a corresponding assignment with at most $t$ faulty two-qubit gate locations that produces the same final data error and measurement outcomes.
\end{proposition}


\begin{proof}

Clifford gates map Pauli operators to Pauli operators under conjugation.
Under the stated adjacency assumption, each preparation, idle,
measurement, or single-qubit gate fault can be propagated to a
neighboring two-qubit gate location. Figure~\ref{equivalence_of_fault}
illustrates the corresponding Pauli propagation, including a
measurement fault represented immediately before measurement.
If several original faults map to the same two-qubit gate location,
their Pauli operators can be multiplied into a single fault at that
location. Thus the resulting assignment has no more nontrivial faulty
two-qubit gate locations than the original execution had faults,
while preserving the final data error and measurement outcomes.
This establishes the coverage needed for verification under the bound
$t$; it does not require a one-to-one correspondence between fault events.
    
\end{proof}

Input data errors and circuit-induced errors are represented separately even though both are Pauli errors.

\subsection{Fault-Tolerant Error Correction}

When faults occur within the circuit, a single round of full syndrome extraction is no longer guaranteed to yield the correct syndrome. To ensure reliable recovery in the presence of such faults, we formally define FTEC. Following~\cite{Tansuwannont2023adaptivesyndrome}, we use the following definition of \emph{weak fault-tolerant error correction}.

\begin{definition}[Weak FTEC]
\label{def:weak-FTEC}
Let $\mathcal{Q}$ be an $[[n,k,d]]$ stabilizer code with $t=\lfloor (d-1)/2\rfloor$. An error correction protocol is \emph{fault-tolerant} up to $t$ faults if the following conditions are satisfied:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Suppose the input data block represents a logical state $|\psi_L\rangle$ corrupted by an error of weight $r$, and the syndrome extraction circuits contain $s$ faults, where $r+s\le t$. Then, after the correction procedure, ideal decoding of the output data block yields exactly the initial logical state $|\psi_L\rangle$.
    \item Suppose the input data block represents a logical state $|\psi_L\rangle$ corrupted by an error of weight $r$, and the syndrome extraction circuits contain $s$ faults, where $r+s\le t$. The output state differs from a valid codeword by an error of weight at most $s$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

The parameter
$t=\lfloor (d-1)/2\rfloor$ denotes the maximum number of faults that the protocol is designed to tolerate and is referred to as the \emph{fault bound}. Ideal decoding refers to fault-free error correction based on the ideal syndrome.

Condition~(1) of Definition~\ref{def:weak-FTEC} guarantees that the output state preserves the same logical information as the input state. Since faults within the circuit can propagate errors to multiple qubits, Condition~(2) additionally provides a rigorous upper bound on the weight of the residual error in the final output state.

A basic syndrome extraction circuit alone cannot prevent fault-induced high-weight Pauli error propagation events. The resulting errors may not be reliably characterized from the syndrome alone, which can cause error correction to fail. To address this issue, flagged fault-tolerant error correction augments a basic syndrome extraction circuit with additional ancilla qubits~\cite{CR18a,CR18b}, called \emph{flag qubits}, such as the qubit labeled $f$ in Fig.~\ref{fig:flagged-circuit}, to detect such error propagation events.  The resulting circuit is referred to as a flagged syndrome extraction circuit. 
A flagged syndrome extraction circuit is required to satisfy the validity condition in Def.~\ref{def:syndrome-extraction-valid}. 



A circuit fault may propagate to multiple data qubits, producing
an error whose weight exceeds the number of faults. Flag qubits
provide additional measurement outcomes to detect such error
amplification. To characterize this property under a general fault
bound $k$, we introduce the following $k$-flag condition.
This condition will later be used to verify the flagged syndrome
extraction circuits required by protocol validity.
\begin{definition}[$k$-Flag Condition] \label{def:flag}
For a given fault bound $k$, a syndrome extraction circuit satisfies
the $k$-flag condition if the following hold:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item With no input data error, whenever an event involving $1\le w\le k$
    circuit faults induces a data error $E_{\mathrm{data}}$ such that
    $\mathrm{wt}_{\min}(E_{\mathrm{data}})>w$, at least one flag
    measurement outcome is $1$.
    \item With no circuit faults, no flag is raised for any input data error.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}




% Therefore, the flag outcomes are included in the measurement record, and the fault-tolerance of a flagged error correction protocol can be verified using the criterion in Def.~\ref{def:weak-FTEC}.

A \textit{flagged fault-tolerant error correction protocol} consists of flagged syndrome extraction circuits and syndrome extraction circuits together with an adaptive control flow. 

The control flow is represented by a finite decision tree (see Fig.~\ref{fig:five-qubit-flagged-protocol}). It determines which syndrome extraction circuit is executed next based on the syndrome and flag outcomes obtained so far and determines when the protocol terminates.
Its paths enumerate all terminating executions.


\begin{definition}[Measurement Record and Residual Error Set]
\label{def:measurement-record-and-residual-error}
For a given execution path, a \emph{measurement record} $m$ is the bit string consisting of all syndrome and flag outcomes observed along that path.
The corresponding residual error set $\mathcal E_m$ consists of all data
Pauli errors before decoding that can arise from executions producing $m$
with input data error of weight $r$ and $s$ circuit faults satisfying
$r+s\le t$.
\end{definition}

We will verify \emph{error-set correctability} by checking
Eq.~\ref{eq:QECC_condition} for each residual error set
$\mathcal E_m$, ensuring that all errors consistent with the
same measurement record are jointly correctable.

Each execution follows a control-flow path, executing syndrome extraction circuits conditioned on intermediate outcomes. Upon termination, a lookup-table decoder maps the observed measurement record $m$ to a Pauli correction applied to the data qubits.

\begin{definition}[Protocol validity]
\label{def:protocol-validity}
A flagged error correction protocol satisfies \emph{protocol validity} if:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Every terminating path satisfies at least one of the following
    conditions under fault bound~$t$:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Condition (1):} The measurement record indicates
        that $t$ faults have been detected, and all residual errors
        associated with the same record have the same ideal syndrome.
        \item \textbf{Condition (2):} At least $w<t$ circuit faults
        have been detected, followed by $t-w+1$ consecutive full
        syndrome extraction rounds with identical syndrome outcomes
        and no raised flags.
        \item \textbf{Condition (3):} For every input data error of
        weight $r$ and every execution with $s$ circuit faults
        satisfying $r+s\le t$, the residual data error has
        minimum weight $\mathrm{wt}_{\min}$ at most $s$.
    \end{enumerate}

    detected-fault lower bound $w$, the full syndrome extraction
    circuit used in its repeated-syndrome suffix satisfies the
    $(t-w)$-flag condition (Def.~\ref{def:flag}).
\end{enumerate}
The flag requirement applies whenever Condition (2) holds, even
if the same path also satisfies Condition (1) or Condition (3).
\end{definition}

without flags. Condition (3) requires the residual error weight
to be bounded by the number of circuit faults.
The following theorem characterizes decoder existence under these
case-specific assumptions and supports the verification algorithm
in Section~\ref{sec:FTEC-verification}.



\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:mains}
Consider a flagged error-correction protocol $\Pi$ for a stabilizer
code with stabilizer group $\cS$, distance $d$, and
$t=\lfloor (d-1)/2 \rfloor$. Assume that every syndrome extraction
circuit used in $\Pi$ is valid according to
Def.~\ref{def:syndrome-extraction-valid}, and that $\Pi$ satisfies
protocol validity (Def.~\ref{def:protocol-validity}).
For each record $m$, let $\mathcal E_m$ be the residual error set
from Def.~\ref{def:measurement-record-and-residual-error}.
Then there exists a lookup-table decoder $D$ such that $(\Pi,D)$
satisfies FTEC if and only if, for every measurement record $m$,
\begin{align}
    \forall E_1,E_2\in\mathcal{E}_m,\qquad
    E_1\cdot  E_2\notin N(\cS)\setminus \cS.
\end{align}
\end{theorem}

By Eq.~\ref{eq:QECC_condition}, the condition in this theorem states
that $\mathcal E_m$ is correctable for every reachable measurement
record $m$, i.e., every record with $\mathcal E_m\neq\varnothing$.

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The complete proof is in Appendix~\ref{app:proof}; Pauli products are
understood up to global phase.

\textbf{($\Rightarrow$)}
If $E_1,E_2\in\mathcal E_m$ differ by a nontrivial logical operator,
the same correction $D(m)$ must work for both. By FTEC,
\[
d\le\mathrm{wt}_{\min}(E_1E_2)
\le\mathrm{wt}_{\min}(E_1D(m))
 +\mathrm{wt}_{\min}(E_2D(m))
\le 2t,
\]
contradicting the code distance.

\textbf{($\Leftarrow$)}
Assume the pairwise condition. Under Condition~(1), errors with
the same record have the same ideal syndrome and are therefore
stabilizer-equivalent; a representative determines the correction.
Under Condition~(2), the repeated unflagged rounds contain a
fault-free round. Removing later faults preserves the record,
and the pairwise condition makes the resulting reference errors
stabilizer-equivalent. Correcting their common class leaves only
faults after that round, bounded by the required flag condition.
Under Condition~(3), no correction is needed before ideal decoding.
Each construction depends only on the measurement record and
satisfies both FTEC requirements.
\end{proof}

The verifier first checks the validity of all syndrome extraction
circuits (Def.~\ref{def:syndrome-extraction-valid}). Only after
this check passes does it perform the correctability check
(Eq.~\ref{eq:QECC_condition}) and protocol-validity check
(Def.~\ref{def:protocol-validity}). Under circuit validity and the
assumed Pauli fault model, symbolic fault propagation represents
the reachable measurement records and residual data errors.
The protocol is accepted as FTEC only if both subsequent checks pass,
by Thm.~\ref{thm:mains}.

We describe the SAT-based, simulation-based, and structural checks
below, followed by their execution order in the overall algorithm.

\subsection{SAT-Based Checks}
\label{subsec:sat-based-checks}


We first define the symbolic objects used by the SAT-based checks.
The verifier analyzes each terminating path of the protocol separately.
Let $\pi$ be a terminating path, and let $\mathcal{L}_\pi$ denote the set of
fault locations along $\pi$. A \emph{fault assignment} is defined as a tuple $\mathcal{F} = (\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{in}}, \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{circ}})$, where $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{in}}$ denotes the input data error and $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{circ}}$ assigns a Pauli fault to each location in $\mathcal{L}_\pi$.

For a data qubit $q_i$, the input is represented by two variables $(q_{i,x}, q_{i,z})$. We introduce an activity variable $a_{i} \in \{0,1\}$ to indicate whether the qubit has an input error:
\begin{equation}
a_{i}
\leftrightarrow
q_{i,x}\vee q_{i,z}.
\end{equation}

For a fault location $\ell\in\mathcal{L}_\pi$ acting on qubits $q_1$ and
$q_2$, the Pauli fault is represented by Boolean variables
$x_{\ell,q_1},z_{\ell,q_1},x_{\ell,q_2},z_{\ell,q_2}\in\{0,1\}$,
where the pair $(x_{\ell,q},z_{\ell,q})$ specifies the Pauli component on
qubit $q$. We introduce an activity variable
$a_\ell\in\{0,1\}$ to indicate whether the fault at $\ell$ is nontrivial:
\begin{equation}
a_\ell
\leftrightarrow
x_{\ell,q_1}\vee z_{\ell,q_1}\vee
x_{\ell,q_2}\vee z_{\ell,q_2}.
\end{equation}

Thus, $a_\ell=1$ exactly when the fault assigned to $\ell$ is not the
identity. The input error weight of $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{in}}$ and the circuit fault count of $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{circ}}$ are then
\[
\mathrm{wt}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{in}})
=
\sum_{i} a_{i},\  
\mathrm{wt}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{circ}})
=
\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}_\pi} a_\ell.
\]
The total fault-assignment weight is defined as
$\mathrm{wt}(\mathcal F)=\mathrm{wt}(\mathcal F_{\mathrm{in}})
+\mathrm{wt}(\mathcal F_{\mathrm{circ}})$,
accounting for both input data errors and circuit faults.
We encode $\mathrm{wt}(\mathcal{F})\le t$ and
$\mathrm{wt}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{circ}})=w$ with cardinality
constraints~\cite{sinz2005towards}.


Given a fault assignment $\mathcal{F}$, the verifier symbolically propagates
the corresponding Pauli faults through the protocol along the terminating path
$\pi$ (see the circuit-level example in Figure~\ref{fig:fault-propagation-circuit}).
This propagation determines three objects:
\[
\Phi_\pi(\mathcal{F}),\qquad
E_\pi(\mathcal{F}),\qquad
m_\pi(\mathcal{F}).
\]
Here, $\Phi_\pi(\mathcal{F})$ is the path constraint specifying that the
syndrome and flag outcomes produced under $\mathcal{F}$ cause the protocol to
follow the terminating path $\pi$; $E_\pi(\mathcal{F})$ is the residual Pauli
error on the data block at the end of $\pi$; and
$m_\pi(\mathcal{F})$ is the measurement record consisting of the syndrome and
flag outcomes available to the decoder.

The symbolic propagation is performed at the level of individual qubits.
For each qubit $q$, the verifier maintains a symbolic Pauli label
\[
Q_q(\mathcal{F})
=
\bigl(
Q_{q,x}(\mathcal{F}),
Q_{q,z}(\mathcal{F})
\bigr),
\]
where $Q_{q,x}(\mathcal{F})$ and $Q_{q,z}(\mathcal{F})$ are Boolean formulas
over the fault variables and represent the symbolic $X$- and $Z$-components
of the Pauli error on $q$. The gate-by-gate update rules for these symbolic
labels are given in Figure~\ref{fig:two-qubit-pauli-rules}.

At the end of the terminating path $\pi$, restricting these symbolic labels to
the data qubits $d_1,\ldots,d_n$ gives the residual data error
\[
E_\pi(\mathcal{F})
=
\bigl(
Q_{d_1,x}(\mathcal{F}),Q_{d_1,z}(\mathcal{F}),
\ldots,
Q_{d_n,x}(\mathcal{F}),Q_{d_n,z}(\mathcal{F})
\bigr).
\]
The same symbolic propagation determines the syndrome and flag outcomes and
hence the measurement record $m_\pi(\mathcal{F})$ and the path constraint
$\Phi_\pi(\mathcal{F})$. Thus, for a fixed terminating path, a fault
assignment is mapped to the symbolic quantities used throughout the SAT
formulation:
\[
\mathcal{F}
\quad\longmapsto\quad
\bigl(
\Phi_\pi(\mathcal{F}),
E_\pi(\mathcal{F}),
m_\pi(\mathcal{F})
\bigr).
\]

This notation makes the dependence of the symbolic quantities on the Boolean
fault variables explicit.

After circuit validity has been established, these symbolic quantities
describe the physical executions within the assumed Pauli fault model.
In particular, assignments satisfying $\Phi_\pi(\mathcal F)$ and
$\mathrm{wt}(\mathcal F)\le t$ generate the residual errors in
$\mathcal E_m$ when $m_\pi(\mathcal F)=m$
(Def.~\ref{def:measurement-record-and-residual-error}).
Because the control flow is a decision tree determined by recorded
outcomes, a complete measurement record determines its terminating path.

Finally, propagation yields the pre-measurement state at $S_3$. Measuring qubit $q$ extracts the Pauli component that anticommutes with the measurement basis: $Q_{q,z}(\mathcal{F})$ for the $X$ basis and $Q_{q,x}(\mathcal{F})$ for the $Z$ basis.

\[
m_1(\mathcal{F}) = Q_{a,z}(\mathcal{F}), \qquad f_1(\mathcal{F}) = Q_{f,x}(\mathcal{F}).

These expressions represent flips relative to the fault-free
measurement outcomes, taken to be zero in the encoding. They represent
the physical measurement bits when the fault-free outcomes are
deterministic and have those reference values, as ensured by circuit
validity. If a circuit has intrinsically nondeterministic fault-free
outcomes, Pauli fault labels alone need not represent every possible
physical measurement record.

\noindent From this circuit-level example, we now construct the protocol-level measurement record and path constraint.

The full measurement record $m_\pi(\mathcal{F}) = \bigl(R_1(\mathcal{F}), \ldots, R_r(\mathcal{F})\bigr)$ collects all $r$ syndrome and flag outcomes along path $\pi$ (where $R_j$ denotes the $j$-th recorded bit, e.g., $R_1 = m_1, R_2 = f_1$). Bitwise equality between two records is encoded as:
\[
m_\pi(\mathcal{F}_1) = m_\pi(\mathcal{F}_2) \quad\equiv\quad \bigwedge_{j=1}^r \bigl(R_j(\mathcal{F}_1)\leftrightarrow R_j(\mathcal{F}_2)\bigr).
\]

Finally, the same recorded syndrome and flag outcomes are substituted into the
branch guards of the control flow to form the path constraint. For example,
suppose the terminating path $\pi$ first takes a branch guarded by
$m_1=0\land f_1=0$ and then a branch guarded by $m_2=1\lor f_2=1$. If symbolic
execution produces the recorded syndrome and flag outcomes
$m_1(\mathcal{F}),f_1(\mathcal{F}),m_2(\mathcal{F}),f_2(\mathcal{F})$, then the corresponding path constraint is
\[
\Phi_\pi(\mathcal{F})
=
\neg m_1(\mathcal{F})
\land
\neg f_1(\mathcal{F})
\land
\bigl(m_2(\mathcal{F})\lor f_2(\mathcal{F})\bigr)
\land \cdots ,
\]
where the remaining conjuncts come from the later branch guards along $\pi$.
Thus, $\Phi_\pi(\mathcal{F})$ is obtained by substituting the symbolic recorded outcomes
into all branch guards along the terminating path and conjoining the resulting
formulas.

At this point, for every terminating path $\pi$ and fault assignment $\mathcal{F}$, we
have Boolean encodings of the three quantities used by the verification
queries: the path constraint $\Phi_\pi(\mathcal{F})$, the residual data error
$E_\pi(\mathcal{F})$, and the measurement record $m_\pi(\mathcal{F})$.

\subsubsection{Correctability Check}
\label{subsec:ftec-check}

After circuit validity has been established, we check correctability
of $\mathcal E_m$ (Def.~\ref{def:measurement-record-and-residual-error})
using Eq.~\ref{eq:QECC_condition}. The subsequent correctability and
protocol-validity checks are evaluated separately.
As in Thm.~\ref{thm:mains}, we ignore global phases and write the
relative Pauli error as the product of two residual data errors.

For each terminating path $\pi$, the verifier searches for two fault
assignments $\mathcal{F}_1$ and $\mathcal{F}_2$ such that both executions follow $\pi$, both have
total fault-assignment weight at most $t$, and both produce the same measurement record, but their
residual data errors violate the quantum error correction condition. The SAT
formula is

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\Psi^{\mathrm{Corr}}_\pi
:=\exists \mathcal F_1,\mathcal F_2.\quad
&\bigwedge_{i=1}^{2}\bigl(
  \Phi_\pi(\mathcal F_i)\land\mathrm{wt}(\mathcal F_i)\le t\bigr)\\
&\land m_\pi(\mathcal F_1)=m_\pi(\mathcal F_2)\\
&\land E_\pi(\mathcal F_1)E_\pi(\mathcal F_2)
  \in N(\cS)\setminus\cS.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

A satisfying assignment witnesses two admissible executions with the
same record but residual errors differing by a nontrivial logical operator.
If the query is unsatisfiable for every terminating path, all reachable
$\mathcal E_m$ are correctable, since each complete record determines its
path. Together with protocol validity, Thm.~\ref{thm:mains} then
guarantees an FTEC decoder.

It remains to encode the last conjunct of $\Psi^{\mathrm{Corr}}_\pi$. Let

$
Q=E_\pi(\mathcal{F}_1) \cdot E_\pi(\mathcal{F}_2).
$
In the binary Pauli representation, $Q$ is obtained by XORing the two residual
data-error vectors. The condition
$
Q\in N(\mathcal S)\setminus\mathcal S
$
is encoded as the conjunction of normalizer membership and stabilizer
nonmembership.

Normalizer membership means that $Q$ commutes with every stabilizer generator
$G_j$. If
$
Q=(Q_{1,x},Q_{1,z},\ldots,Q_{n,x},Q_{n,z}),
$
then this is encoded by

\begin{equation} \label{eq:Normalize-membership}
    \begin{aligned}
       \mathrm{Norm}(Q,\mathcal{S}) =  \bigwedge_j
\left(
\bigoplus_{i=1}^n
\Bigl(
(Q_{i,x} \land G_{j,i,z})
\oplus
(Q_{i,z} \land G_{j,i,x})
\Bigr)
=0
\right).
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}



Stabilizer nonmembership is encoded using a fixed set of logical Pauli operators
$
\mathcal{L} = \{L_1,\ldots,L_{2k}\},
$
precomputed from the stabilizer generators of the code.
Since the previous constraint already enforces $Q\in N(\mathcal S)$, the
condition $Q\notin\mathcal S$ is equivalent to saying that $Q$ has nontrivial
logical action. Equivalently, $Q$ must anticommute with at least one logical
Pauli generator. Thus the verifier encodes stabilizer nonmembership by

\begin{equation} \label{eq:stabiizer-nonmembership}
    \begin{aligned}
       \mathrm{Stab}_{\mathrm{non}}(Q, \mathcal{L}) = \bigvee_{h=1}^{2k}
\left(
\bigoplus_{i=1}^n
\Bigl(
(Q_{i,x} \land L_{h,i,z})
\oplus
(Q_{i,z} \land L_{h,i,x})
\Bigr)
=1
\right).
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}



Together $\mathrm{Norm}(Q,\mathcal{S}) \wedge \mathrm{Stab}_{\mathrm{non}}(Q, \mathcal{L})$ encode $Q\in N(\cS)\setminus\cS$,
completing the SAT query.



\subsubsection{Check for Condition~(1) of Def.~\ref{def:protocol-validity} used in Thm.~\ref{thm:mains}}
\label{subsec:cf-validity-condition1}

We next describe the SAT check for the assumption of Condition~(1) in Def.~\ref{def:protocol-validity}, as required by Thm.~\ref{thm:mains}. We must check that if a path satisfies Condition~(1), any executions that produce the same measurement record leave residual data errors with the same ideal syndrome. Therefore, the verifier searches for a counterexample: two executions that follow the same terminating path and produce the same measurement record, but whose residual data errors have different ideal syndromes.

For each terminating path $\pi$ intended to satisfy Condition (1), the verifier
checks satisfiability of the formula

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\Psi^{\mathrm{CF1}}_\pi
:=\exists \mathcal F_1,\mathcal F_2.\quad
&\bigwedge_{i=1}^{2}\bigl(
  \Phi_\pi(\mathcal F_i)\land\mathrm{wt}(\mathcal F_i)\le t\bigr)\\
&\land m_\pi(\mathcal F_1)=m_\pi(\mathcal F_2)\\
&\land \mathrm{syn}(E_\pi(\mathcal F_1))
 \ne \mathrm{syn}(E_\pi(\mathcal F_2)).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The conjuncts require both executions to follow the same terminating
path and satisfy the fault bound, equate their measurement records,
and require their residual data errors to have different ideal syndromes.

The ideal syndrome is computed from the binary Pauli representation by the
usual symplectic inner product with the stabilizer generators. For a data error
$
E=(E_{1,x},E_{1,z},\ldots,E_{n,x},E_{n,z}),
$
the syndrome bit with respect to a stabilizer generator $G_j$ is

\[
\mathrm{syn}_j(E) = \bigoplus_{i=1}^n \bigl( (E_{i,x} \land G_{j,i,z}) \oplus (E_{i,z} \land G_{j,i,x}) \bigr).
\]
Thus the inequality of 
$
\mathrm{syn}(E_\pi(\mathcal{F}_1))
$ and $
\mathrm{syn}(E_\pi(\mathcal{F}_2))
$
is encoded as
\[
\bigvee_j
\left(
\mathrm{syn}_j(E_\pi(\mathcal{F}_1))
\oplus
\mathrm{syn}_j(E_\pi(\mathcal{F}_2))
\right).
\]

If $\Psi^{\mathrm{CF1}}_\pi$ is satisfiable, then the same measurement record
can correspond to two different ideal syndromes, so Condition (1) of
Def.~\ref{def:protocol-validity} fails on $\pi$. If the formula is
unsatisfiable, the record uniquely determines the ideal syndrome
for all executions on $\pi$ within the fault bound.

\subsubsection{Control-Flow Validity: Condition (3)}
\label{subsec:cf-validity-condition3}


We next describe the SAT check for Condition (3) of
Def.~\ref{def:protocol-validity}. This condition applies to terminating
paths for which the decoder applies the trivial correction. For an input
error of weight $r$ and $w$ circuit faults with $r+w\le t$, the residual
data error must have minimum weight at most $w$.

In the following query, $w$ is fixed, while
$r$ abbreviates $\mathrm{wt}(\mathcal F_{\mathrm{in}})$ for the
existentially chosen fault assignment $\mathcal F$; it is not
an additional fixed parameter. Thus all input error weights satisfying
$r+w\le t$ are considered.
For each terminating path $\pi$ intended to satisfy Condition (3), and for each
$w=0,\ldots,t$, the verifier searches for a fault assignment that violates this
requirement:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\Psi^{\mathrm{CF3}}_{\pi,w}
:=\exists\mathcal F.\quad
&\Phi_\pi(\mathcal F)\land r+w\le t\\
&\land\mathrm{wt}(\mathcal F_{\mathrm{in}})=r
\land\mathrm{wt}(\mathcal F_{\mathrm{circ}})=w\\
&\land\mathrm{wt}_{\min}(E_\pi(\mathcal F))>w.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The constraints require the input error weight $r$ and the circuit fault
count $w$ to satisfy $r+w\le t$, while the path constraint requires the
execution to follow $\pi$. The final conjunct searches for a residual
data error whose minimum weight exceeds $w$, even after multiplying by
stabilizers.


Recall from Eq.~(\ref{eq:wt_min}) that 
$ \mathrm{wt}_{\min}(E) = \min_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\mathrm{wt}(E\cdot S). $ 
To encode the condition $\mathrm{wt}_{\min}(E_\pi(\mathcal{F})) > w$, we must assert that there exists no Pauli string $P$ of weight $\mathrm{wt}(P) \leq w$ such that $E_\pi(\mathcal{F})\cdot P \in \mathcal{S}$. A Pauli string belongs to the stabilizer group if it commutes with all stabilizer generators and logical operators. We encode this using normalizer membership (Eq.~\ref{eq:Normalize-membership}) and stabilizer nonmembership (Eq.~\ref{eq:stabiizer-nonmembership}):

\begin{equation} \label{eq:minimum-weight}
    \begin{aligned}
      \mathrm{wt}_{\min}(E_\pi(\mathcal{F}))>w \ \Longleftrightarrow \bigwedge_{P : \mathrm{wt}(P) \leq w} \left(\neg\mathrm{Norm}(P \cdot E_\pi(\mathcal{F}), \mathcal{S}) \lor \mathrm{Stab}_{\mathrm{non}}(P \cdot E_\pi(\mathcal{F}), \mathcal{L})\right)
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

% b_1(c_1,\mathcal{F})+b_2(c_1,\mathcal{F})+b_3(c_1,\mathcal{F})>w.
% \]
% To encode $\mathrm{wt}_{\min}(E_\pi(\mathcal{F}))>w$, the verifier requires this constraint for both choices of $c_1$:
% \[
% \bigl(b_1(0,\mathcal{F})+b_2(0,\mathcal{F})+b_3(0,\mathcal{F})>w\bigr) \land \bigl(b_1(1,\mathcal{F})+b_2(1,\mathcal{F})+b_3(1,\mathcal{F})>w\bigr).
% \]
% For $c_1=0$, this checks the weight of $E_\pi(\mathcal{F})$ itself. For $c_1=1$, this checks the weight after multiplying by the stabilizer $ZZI$.\qed
% \end{example}


If $\Psi^{\mathrm{CF3}}_{\pi,w}$ is satisfiable for some $w$, then there is a
$w$-fault execution on $\pi$ whose residual data error has minimum  weight larger
than $w$, so Condition (3) of Def.~\ref{def:protocol-validity} fails on
$\pi$. If the formula is unsatisfiable for every $w=0,\ldots,t$, then
Condition (3) holds on $\pi$.

\subsubsection{$(t-w)$-Flag-Condition Check}
\label{subsec:tw-flag-condition-check}

We check the flag-condition requirement in Def.~\ref{def:protocol-validity}
only for terminating paths satisfying Condition (2) of
Def.~\ref{def:protocol-validity}. For
such a path, the structural check for Condition (2)
determines the detected-fault lower bound $w$ on the path. Protocol validity
then requires the full syndrome extraction circuit used in the
repeated-syndrome suffix to satisfy the $(t-w)$-flag condition.



Let $C$ be this full syndrome extraction circuit, and let $F_C$ be the
set of flag qubits in $C$. For a fault assignment $\mathcal{F}$ on $C$,
symbolic Pauli propagation produces a residual data error
$E_C(\mathcal{F})$ and, for each flag qubit $q\in F_C$, a recorded flag
outcome $f_q(\mathcal{F})$. Let $k=t-w$, and abbreviate
$s=\mathrm{wt}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{circ}})$. We encode both violations of the
$k$-flag condition in a single SAT query:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:flag-check}
\begin{aligned}
\Psi^{\mathrm{flag}}_{C,k}:=\exists\mathcal{F}.\quad
&\bigl(s=0\land\bigvee_{q\in F_C}f_q(\mathcal{F})\bigr)\\
&\lor\bigl(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{in}}=I\land 1\le s\le k
\land\neg\bigvee_{q\in F_C}f_q(\mathcal{F})
\land\mathrm{wt}_{\min}(E_C(\mathcal{F}))>s\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The first disjunct detects a raised flag when there are no circuit faults,
allowing arbitrary input data errors. The second disjunct assumes no input
data error ($\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{in}}=I$) and detects a fault-induced
data error with minimum weight greater than the number $s$ of circuit
faults, without any raised flag, for some $1\le s\le k$.

As in the Condition (3) check, the constraint
$\mathrm{wt}_{\min}(E_C(\mathcal{F}))>s$ is encoded using
Eq.~\eqref{eq:minimum-weight} with $w$ replaced by $s$.
This encoding enumerates Pauli operators $P$ of weight at most $s$
and checks that $P E_C(\mathcal{F})$ does not belong to the stabilizer group.

If $\Psi^{\mathrm{flag}}_{C,k}$ is satisfiable, at least one of the two
flag-condition requirements is violated. The circuit satisfies the required
$k$-flag condition if and only if $\Psi^{\mathrm{flag}}_{C,k}$ is
unsatisfiable.

\subsubsection{Circuit Correctness Check}
\label{subsec:circuit-correctness-check}

The first part of the circuit validity check verifies
the correctness condition in
Def.~\ref{def:syndrome-extraction-correct}.
This check is performed for each syndrome extraction
circuit in the protocol and must hold for every
possible input Pauli error on the data block.

Let $\cC$ be a circuit intended to measure
Pauli strings $\{P_i\}_{i=1}^{m}$.
The verifier symbolically propagates an input
Pauli error $E_{\mathrm{in}}$ on the data qubits
through $\cC$ without circuit faults.
Let $E_{\mathrm{out}}$ be the resulting data error,
and let $E_{\mathrm{anc}}$ be the propagated
ancilla error.

For each designated syndrome ancilla $i$, define
\[
    R_i(E_{\mathrm{in}})
    =E_{\mathrm{anc}}\odot A_i,
\]
where $A_i$ is its measurement observable.
Thus, $R_i(E_{\mathrm{in}})$ represents the
measurement-bit flip induced by the input Pauli
error relative to the fault-free reference outcome.

The verifier searches for an input error whose propagated data error
is not stabilizer-equivalent to the input or whose measured syndrome
differs from the ideal syndrome:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\Psi^{\mathrm{corr}}_{\cC}
:=
\exists \mathcal{F}. \quad \mathrm{wt}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{circ}}) = 0 
\quad & 
 \land \Bigl(
E_{\mathrm{out}}(\mathcal{F})\not\sim E_{\mathrm{in}}(\mathcal{F})
\;\vee\;
\bigvee_{i=1}^{m}
R_i(E_{\mathrm{in}}(\mathcal{F}))
\neq
E_{\mathrm{in}}(\mathcal{F})\odot P_i
\Bigr).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here $\odot$ denotes the binary symplectic
product from Eq.~\ref{eq:symp_ip}, and $\sim$
denotes equivalence up to a data stabilizer and Pauli phase,
checked using Eqs.~\ref{eq:Normalize-membership}
and~\ref{eq:stabiizer-nonmembership}.

If $\Psi^{\mathrm{corr}}_{\cC}$ is satisfiable,
the satisfying assignment gives an input
Pauli error witnessing that $\cC$ is not
correct. If it is unsatisfiable, then
$\cC$ satisfies
Def.~\ref{def:syndrome-extraction-correct}.

This check constrains only the designated
syndrome ancillas; no syndrome correctness
condition is imposed on the remaining ancillas.
Their measurement determinism is verified
separately.
\subsection{Simulation and Direct-Inspection Checks}
\label{subsec:simulation-direct-checks}

The SAT checks above verify properties naturally
expressed as the absence of a counterexample.
The remaining checks use different mechanisms.
The second part of circuit validity verification
uses fault-free stabilizer-tableau simulation,
while Condition (2) of
Def.~\ref{def:protocol-validity}
is checked directly from the structure of
the terminating path.

\subsubsection{Circuit Determinism Check}
\label{subsec:circuit-validity-check}

The second part of the circuit validity check
verifies the determinism condition in
Def.~\ref{def:syndrome-extraction-deterministic}.
Unlike the correctness check, this check
considers all ancilla measurements, including
those not designated for syndrome extraction.

By Theorem~\ref{thm:syndrome-extraction-valid}, once circuit correctness
is verified, validity reduces to joint stabilizer and logical preservation,
both checked by stabilizer-tableau simulation.

The verifier propagates the joint data-ancilla
stabilizer generators through the Clifford
gates and checks whether the resulting signed
stabilizer group equals the initial joint
stabilizer group. It also verifies that each
logical Pauli generator is preserved up to
an element of the joint stabilizer group.

Table~\ref{tab:stabilizer-evolution} illustrates
the stabilizer propagation underlying this
check by tracking the evolution of the joint
data-ancilla stabilizer generators through
the Clifford gates. The verifier performs
the same computation using Stim's stabilizer-tableau
simulation.

Joint stabilizer preservation ensures that
all ancilla measurements have the intended
deterministic outcomes in the error-free
execution, while logical preservation ensures
that the encoded logical state remains
unchanged. Since Clifford circuits propagate
Pauli errors to Pauli errors, an arbitrary
input data Pauli error can only change the
ancilla measurement outcomes by fixed
commutation signs. Consequently, all ancilla
outcomes are determined independently of
the encoded state and can serve as well-defined
entries in the measurement record.

If either preservation condition fails,
the circuit does not satisfy the validity
characterization in
Theorem~\ref{thm:syndrome-extraction-valid}
and is rejected by the verifier.
\subsubsection{Control-Flow Validity: Condition (2)}
\label{subsec:cf-validity-condition2}

Condition (2) of Def.~\ref{def:protocol-validity} is checked by direct
inspection of terminating paths. This condition applies to paths that terminate
after a repeated full-syndrome suffix. For each such path $\pi$, the verifier
infers a detected-fault lower bound $w$ from the preceding measurement
record using the counting rule of~\cite[Sections~2.2 and~3.1]{CB18}.
In particular, when comparing consecutive unflagged full-syndrome
rounds, a syndrome change increments the counter by one only if the
preceding comparison did not increment it.
It then checks that the terminating suffix contains $t-w+1$ consecutive full
syndrome extraction rounds whose recorded syndrome outcomes are required by the
branch guards to be identical, and whose flag outcomes are required to be zero.

This check is structural: it does not search over fault assignments. It only
verifies that the control flow has the form required by Condition (2). The
reliability of the repeated syndrome follows from full syndrome extraction
circuit validity and the $(t-w)$-flag check in
Section~\ref{subsec:tw-flag-condition-check}.
\subsection{Overall Algorithm}
\label{subsec:overall-algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:ftec-verification} checks circuit validity first.
If it fails, the protocol is rejected without running the remaining
checks. Otherwise, correctability and protocol validity are checked
separately. All three properties must pass for FTEC acceptance,
by Thm.~\ref{thm:mains}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]

\DontPrintSemicolon
\SetAlgoLined
\SetAlgoNoEnd
\caption{Verification of a flagged error correction protocol}
\label{alg:ftec-verification}
\KwIn{Stabilizer code $\mathcal Q$, flagged protocol $\Pi$, fault bound $t$}
\KwOut{$(v_{\mathrm{circ}},v_{\mathrm{prot}},v_{\mathrm{corr}})$; $\bot$ means not evaluated}

\ForEach{syndrome extraction circuit $C$ used in $\Pi$}{
    \lIf{\textsc{CircuitValidity}$(C)$ fails}{\Return $(\mathrm{false},\bot,\bot)$}
}
$v_{\mathrm{circ}}\gets\mathrm{true}$; 
$v_{\mathrm{corr}}\gets\mathrm{true}$;
$v_{\mathrm{prot}}\gets\mathrm{true}$\;
\ForEach{terminating path $\pi\in\Pi$}{
    \lIf{$\Psi^{\mathrm{Corr}}_\pi$ is SAT}{$v_{\mathrm{corr}}\gets\mathrm{false}$}
}


\ForEach{terminating path $\pi\in\Pi$}{
    \lIf{\textsc{ControlFlowValidity}$(\pi,t)$ fails}{$v_{\mathrm{prot}}\gets\mathrm{false}$}
    \If{$\pi$ satisfies the path condition (2) with detected-fault lower bound $w$}{
        Let $C$ be the full syndrome extraction circuit used in $\pi$'s repeated-syndrome suffix\;
        \lIf{\textsc{FlagCondition}$(C,t-w)$ fails}{$v_{\mathrm{prot}}\gets\mathrm{false}$}
    }
}

\Return $(v_{\mathrm{circ}},v_{\mathrm{prot}},v_{\mathrm{corr}})$
\end{algorithm}

The subroutines in Algorithm~\ref{alg:ftec-verification} refer to the checks
defined above. \textsc{CircuitValidity} checks
Def.~\ref{def:syndrome-extraction-valid} using stabilizer-tableau simulation and a SAT-based check.


\textsc{ControlFlowValidity} checks whether at least one of the three
conditions in Def.~\ref{def:protocol-validity} holds for the path $\pi$.
It returns success if any condition is satisfied, and failure otherwise.
For Condition (1), it checks that the record indicates $t$ detected
faults and that $\Psi^{\mathrm{CF1}}_\pi$ is unsatisfiable.
For Condition (2), it checks that the path has detected $w<t$ faults
and terminates after $t-w+1$ consecutive full syndrome extraction rounds
with identical syndrome outcomes and no raised flags.
For Condition (3), it checks that all required
$\Psi^{\mathrm{CF3}}_{\pi,w}$ queries are unsatisfiable.
The required $(t-w)$-flag condition is checked separately by
\textsc{FlagCondition} in Algorithm~\ref{alg:ftec-verification} for every
path satisfying Condition (2), even if another path condition also holds.

\begin{theorem}[Verification correctness]
\label{thm:verification-correctness}
Under the assumed Pauli fault model,
Algorithm~\ref{alg:ftec-verification} correctly verifies
circuit validity, protocol validity, and correctability
of the residual error sets.

If circuit validity fails, the remaining checks are
not performed. Otherwise, the algorithm returns
$(v_{\mathrm{circ}},v_{\mathrm{prot}},v_{\mathrm{corr}})$,
where each Boolean result indicates whether the
corresponding property holds.

If all three properties hold, there exists a
lookup-table decoder $D$ such that $(\Pi,D)$
satisfies FTEC.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The circuit-validity check establishes
Def.~\ref{def:syndrome-extraction-valid}.
Once circuit validity holds, the SAT queries
$\Psi^{\mathrm{Corr}}_\pi$ characterize violations
of correctability for each terminating path,
while the control-flow and flag-condition checks
establish protocol validity.

Thus the algorithm correctly determines the
three properties. If all three hold,
Thm.~\ref{thm:mains} guarantees the existence
of an FTEC lookup-table decoder.
\end{proof}

The table entries follow four blocks.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Circuit Validity:} \emph{Syndrome extraction bugs} counts QASM circuits that fail circuit validity (Def.~\ref{def:syndrome-extraction-valid}). The Stim and CMS rows report their respective times and peak memory.
    \item \textbf{Protocol Validity:} \emph{Control-flow bugs} counts paths that fail the termination requirements of Def.~\ref{def:protocol-validity}; \emph{flag bugs} counts circuits that fail the applicable flag condition (Def.~\ref{def:flag}). The CMS rows report resource costs; \emph{Time} is left blank pending a measurement that excludes circuit-validity costs.
    \item \textbf{Correctability:} \emph{Correctability failures} counts paths with counterexamples in the symbolic fault model. The CMS rows report resource costs; \emph{Time} combines encoding/export time with summed solver wall times. In both this block and Protocol Validity, \#Vars and \#Clauses give the min--max formula sizes across SAT instances.
    \item \textbf{Overall Verification Result:} \emph{Circuit valid}, \emph{Protocol valid}, and \emph{Correctable} correspond to $(v_{\mathrm{circ}},v_{\mathrm{prot}},v_{\mathrm{corr}})$ in Algorithm~\ref{alg:ftec-verification}. If circuit validity fails, the latter two entries are $\bot$. Separate diagnostics were nevertheless collected for such protocols; they do not establish physical correctability or imply that the algorithm proceeded past an invalid circuit. \emph{FTEC accepted} is Yes exactly when all three checks pass.
\end{itemize}







Highlighting specific findings from Table~\ref{tab:combined-results}, the $[[5,1,3]]$ code with the $[2,2]$ and $[2,2]^T$ optimizations failed the Stim determinism check. However, the corrected $[[5,1,3]]$ version, denoted as $[2,2]^T$ fix$_2$, satisfied all determinism requirements. Furthermore, the $[[17,1,5]]$ and $[[19,1,5]]$ protocols from~\cite{Du24} both failed the flag condition (Def.~\ref{def:flag}).

The independent symbolic correctability diagnostics found counterexamples
on paths of the $[[5,1,3]]$ protocols with $[2,2]$ and $[2,2]^T$
optimizations, including the corrected syndrome-circuit variant
($[2,2]^T$ fix$_2$). They also found three such paths in the
$[[17,1,5]]$ protocol from~\cite{Du24}. For the circuit-invalid
$[2,2]$ and $[2,2]^T$ variants, these are diagnostic findings rather
than physical correctability conclusions. All three verification
properties are required for FTEC acceptance.
 
 
We illustrate two distinct failure modes.
Example 1 demonstrates a circuit-validity failure: a syndrome extraction
circuit violates Def.~\ref{def:syndrome-extraction-deterministic}.
Example 2 demonstrates a correctability failure: two admissible
executions with the same reachable measurement record leave residual
errors whose product is a nontrivial logical operator.


\textbf{Example 1.} Figure~\ref{fig:[[5,1,3]]_[2,2]^T} illustrates the flagged syndrome extraction circuit for the $[[5,1,3]]$ code utilizing the $[2,2]^T$ optimization from [LL25]. Let $U$ denote the overall unitary evolution of this circuit, 
$\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{joint}} = \langle g_0, \dots, g_9 \rangle$ be the initial stabilizer
group of the joint data-ancilla system, and $\mathcal{S}'_{\mathrm{joint}} = \langle g'_0, \dots, g'_9 \rangle$ be the stabilizer
group after $U$ is applied.

The complete evolution of these generators is detailed in Table~\ref{tab:stabilizer-evolution-exmple}. As shown in the table, the transformed generator $g'_4 = +X_{D_1} Z_{D_2} Z_{D_3} X_{D_4}X_{A_{x,1}} Z_{A_{x,2}} Z_{A_{x,3}} Z_{A_{x,4}}$ anticommutes with the initial generator $g_7= +X_{A_{x,4}}$. Because all elements within a stabilizer group must mutually commute, $g'_4$ cannot belong to $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{joint}}$, establishing that $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{joint}} \neq \mathcal{S}'_{\mathrm{joint}}$. Consequently, the measurement outcome of the ancilla qubit $A_{x,4}$ becomes nondeterministic. This directly violates Definition~\ref{def:syndrome-extraction-deterministic}, demonstrating that this protocol is not a valid syndrome extraction circuit.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Flagged error correction and fault distinguishability.}
Flagged error correction reduces ancilla overhead by using flag outcomes to identify potentially harmful fault propagation~\cite{CR18a,CB18}. Subsequent constructions further reduce resource costs through adaptive measurements, parallel extraction, and flag sharing~\cite{low-depth,Liou25,Du24}. Fault distinguishability is central to these designs. Pato et al.~\cite{PTH+24} study distinguishable fault sets and develop tools for checking distinguishability and constructing lookup-table decoders. Their criterion requires fault combinations with the same error syndrome and cumulative flag vector to produce stabilizer-equivalent data errors. They also develop adaptive decoding procedures. Our work addresses the verification of a given adaptive protocol: we analyze the complete noisy measurement records produced along its execution paths and characterize when these records admit a weak-FTEC decoder, under explicit circuit- and protocol-validity conditions. The characterization connects error-set correctability to both logical-state preservation and fault containment.

\paragraph{Formal verification of quantum error correction.}
Chen et al.~\cite{chen2025verifying} develop symbolic execution techniques for verifying fault tolerance of quantum error-correction gadgets. Their framework can abstract classical decoders through assertions on their outputs, avoiding execution of a concrete decoder implementation. Huang et al.~\cite{10.1145/3729293} introduce Veri-QEC, which uses program logic and SMT solving to verify quantum error-correcting programs. Their framework can also derive conditions that a decoder must satisfy. These approaches therefore support reasoning about recovery without requiring an explicit lookup table.

Our verification target is whether the measurement information collected by a protocol admits a suitable decoder. We express this requirement directly through the residual-error sets associated with reachable records. After establishing circuit validity, the verifier searches for two bounded-fault executions with the same record whose residual errors differ by a nontrivial logical operator. Protocol-validity checks separately establish the termination and flag conditions needed to turn the absence of such pairs into a weak-FTEC guarantee. Thus, the correctability queries contain fault variables and execution constraints, but no decoder implementation or decoder-output variables.

\paragraph{Other automated quantum verification techniques.}
Automated quantum verification also includes SMT-based circuit analysis, such as SymQV~\cite{BauerMarquartLS23}, automata-based symbolic execution~\cite{ChenCLLTY23,ChenCLLT23,autoq2}, and abstract interpretation~\cite{yu2021quantum,perdrix2008quantum}. These techniques provide methods for representing quantum states and checking program properties. Our framework instead exploits Clifford Pauli propagation and stabilizer structure to formulate a specialized verification problem over pairs of faulty executions. It checks whether an adaptive protocol collects sufficient information for fault-tolerant recovery, rather than whether a fault-free circuit implements a specified operation.



\section{Conclusion}

In this paper, we presented a formal verification framework for fault-tolerant flagged error correction protocols. 
Rather than explicitly constructing a decoder, we established that, when the extraction circuits and protocol are valid, an FTEC decoder exists if and only if the residual error set for every reachable measurement record is correctable.
Our framework first checks circuit validity and then separately verifies protocol validity and error-set correctability using SAT-based and structural checks. A protocol is accepted as FTEC only when all three properties hold. The verifier also identifies concrete design flaws in flagged protocols.
In particular, it can help detect errors that are difficult to uncover by
manual verification, especially in optimized protocols with reduced circuit
depth, flag-qubit sharing, or adaptive control flow.

In future work, we plan to extend our Pauli-tracking encoding to other
fault-tolerant gadgets and broader quantum computing architectures, such as
measurement-based quantum computation.

\section{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:mains}}
\label{app:proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:mains}]
All Pauli products are considered up to global phase. Throughout the proof,
$r$ denotes the input error weight and $s$ the number of circuit faults,
with $r+s\le t$, as in Def.~\ref{def:measurement-record-and-residual-error}.
Since every extraction circuit is valid by the theorem's premise,
the measurement records and residual errors in $\mathcal E_m$
correspond to the executions under the assumed fault model.
For a fixed measurement record $m$, the decoder must use the same correction
for every execution represented in $\mathcal E_m$.

\paragraph{($\Rightarrow$).}
Suppose there exists a lookup-table decoder $D$ such that $(\Pi,D)$
satisfies FTEC. Fix a measurement record $m$ and two residual errors
$E_1,E_2\in\mathcal E_m$. Since the records are identical, the decoder
applies the same correction $C=D(m)$ to both errors.
The two FTEC requirements ensure that each corrected error is equivalent,
up to a stabilizer, to an error of weight at most the number of circuit
faults in its execution, and hence at most $t$.

Suppose, for a contradiction, that
$E_1\cdot E_2\in N(\cS)\setminus\cS$.
A nontrivial logical operator has minimum weight at least $d$.
On the other hand, minimum weight is subadditive, so
\[
\begin{aligned}
 d
 &\le \mathrm{wt}_{\min}(E_1\cdot E_2)\\
 &=\mathrm{wt}_{\min}\bigl((E_1\cdot C)(E_2\cdot C)\bigr)\\
 &\le\mathrm{wt}_{\min}(E_1\cdot C)+\mathrm{wt}_{\min}(E_2\cdot C)
 \le 2t,
\end{aligned}
\]
contradicting $d>2t$. Therefore,
$E_1\cdot E_2\notin N(\cS)\setminus\cS$.

\paragraph{($\Leftarrow$).}
Assume that, for every measurement record $m$,
\[
 \forall E_1,E_2\in\mathcal E_m,\qquad
 E_1\cdot E_2\notin N(\cS)\setminus\cS.
\]
We choose a correction for each reachable record according to the
path condition of its path and prove the two FTEC requirements.

\paragraph{Path condition (1).}
By protocol validity, all errors in $\mathcal E_m$ have the same
ideal syndrome. Choose any representative $C\in\mathcal E_m$ as the
decoder output. For every $E\in\mathcal E_m$, equal syndromes imply
$E\cdot C\in N(\cS)$. The assumed pairwise condition then gives
$E\cdot C\in\cS$. Thus recovery preserves the original logical state
and leaves minimum residual weight $0\le s$, satisfying both FTEC
requirements.

\paragraph{Path condition (2).}
Let $w$ be the certified lower bound on the number of circuit faults
before the repeated suffix. The suffix consists of $t-w+1$ full syndrome
extraction rounds with identical syndrome outcomes and no flags.
For any execution with $s$ circuit faults, at most $s-w\le t-w$
faults occur in this suffix. Therefore, at least one of these rounds is
fault-free and reports the ideal syndrome of its input data error.

Consider the execution obtained by keeping the same input error and all
faults before this fault-free round, and removing all faults after it.
The measurement outcomes through the fault-free round are unchanged.
Every subsequent round is now fault-free, so circuit validity gives
the same ideal syndrome as the fault-free round, and the no-fault
flag property ensures that no flags are raised even in the presence
of the incoming data error. Since the original suffix also had this
syndrome in every round and no raised flags, the outcomes of each
subsequent round agree with the original execution. Inductively, all
later branch guards select the same branches, so the modified execution
follows the same terminating path and produces the same record $m$.
Removing faults cannot increase the fault count, so its residual
error, equivalent to the data error at the fault-free round, belongs
to $\mathcal E_m$.

For a fixed record $m$, all modified executions constructed this way
leave errors with the same ideal syndrome. For any two such errors
$E_1,E_2\in\mathcal E_m$, we therefore have
$E_1\cdot E_2\in N(\cS)$. The assumed pairwise condition implies
$E_1\cdot E_2\in\cS$. Choose one of these errors as the correction $C$.
This single choice depends only on $m$ and cancels, up to stabilizers,
the error at a fault-free suffix round in every compatible execution.

Consequently, for an actual residual error $E\in\mathcal E_m$,
$E\cdot C$ is equivalent up to stabilizers to the error induced by
faults after that fault-free round. There are at most $s-w\le t-w$
circuit faults in the entire suffix, so each remaining round contains
at most $t-w$ faults and is covered by the $(t-w)$-flag condition.
By the no-fault
flag requirement, the flag outcomes vanish for any input data error
in the absence of circuit faults. Since Pauli propagation and the
recorded flag bits are linear in the Pauli error representation,
adding an incoming data error does not change the flag outcomes
caused by the circuit faults. Hence each unflagged round with its
incoming error removed is also unflagged, and its fault-induced data
error has minimum weight at most the number of faults in that round.
Circuit correctness preserves incoming data errors up to stabilizers.
Minimum weight is subadditive, so summing the bounds for the rounds
after the fault-free round gives
\[
 \mathrm{wt}_{\min}(E\cdot C)\le s-w\le s.
\]
This proves FTEC~(2). Since the remaining error is relative to the
original logical state and has minimum weight at most $s\le t$,
ideal decoding removes it, proving FTEC~(1).

\paragraph{Path condition (3).}
Choose the identity correction, $D(m)=I$.
By Condition~(3) of protocol validity, every execution with
$r+s\le t$ producing $m$ has residual error $E\in\mathcal E_m$ satisfying
\[
 \mathrm{wt}_{\min}(E)\le s.
\]
This directly establishes FTEC~(2). Because $s\le t$, ideal decoding
corrects this residual error and recovers the original logical state,
establishing FTEC~(1).

If a terminating path satisfies more than one condition, any one of
the corresponding constructions may be used: each produces a
correction depending only on $m$ and satisfies both FTEC requirements
for all executions with that record. Thus a single lookup-table
decoder can be chosen for all reachable records, completing the proof.
\end{proof}

\section{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:syndrome-extraction-valid}}
\label{app:proof-syndrome-extraction}

\begin{proof}
Assume first that $U$ is valid. By
Def.~\ref{def:syndrome-extraction-deterministic}, in the error-free
execution the data remains in the codespace, each ancilla has its
prescribed measurement outcome, and the encoded logical state is
unchanged up to global phase. Since the ancilla observables $A_i$
have definite outcomes, the joint state remains in the simultaneous
$+1$ eigenspace of $\mathcal S_{\mathrm{joint}}$.
Thus $U$ preserves this joint codespace, implying
$U\mathcal S_{\mathrm{joint}}U^\dagger=\mathcal S_{\mathrm{joint}}$
with Pauli signs retained. Preservation of the logical state also
implies that $UL_iU^\dagger$ and $L_i$ act identically on the joint
codespace, so $(UL_iU^\dagger)L_i\in\mathcal S_{\mathrm{joint}}$
for each $i$. Correctness follows from validity by definition.

Conversely, assume that $U$ is correct and satisfies the two
preservation conditions. Signed equality of the joint stabilizer
groups ensures that the error-free output remains in the joint
codespace. In particular, all ancilla observables $A_i$ have
deterministic $+1$ outcomes, and the output data remains in the
data codespace. The logical-operator condition implies that $U$
preserves the action of every logical Pauli generator on this
codespace; since these generators act irreducibly on the encoded
degrees of freedom, $U$ acts as the identity up to global phase.
For any input data Pauli error $E$, Clifford conjugation gives
a fixed propagated Pauli error, so the ancilla outcomes differ
from their error-free values by fixed commutation signs,
independently of the encoded state. Correctness additionally
ensures the designated syndrome outcomes and preservation of
the input data error up to stabilizers. Hence $U$ is
deterministic and correct, and therefore valid.
\end{proof}
