\begin{abstract}
Fault-tolerant syndrome extraction protocols based on flag qubits provide a resource-efficient approach to quantum error correction. Verifying their correctness, however, is challenging because fault tolerance depends not only on fault propagation within the extraction circuits, but also on the decoder's ability to correctly interpret measurement outcomes and recover from different fault events.

In this work, we show that explicit decoder construction is unnecessary for verifying flagged syndrome extraction protocols. We prove that a fault-tolerant decoder exists if and only if all fault events consistent with the same measured generalized syndrome produce degenerate residual errors. This result enables the verification of decoder existence without constructing the decoder itself.

Based on this characterization, we develop a formal verification framework that symbolically models Pauli fault propagation, measurement outcomes, and protocol control flow. We encode violations of the distinguishability property as satisfiability queries and automatically search for counterexamples using SAT solving.
We evaluate our framework on a collection of representative flagged syndrome extraction protocols from the literature. Our analysis formally validates several existing constructions and uncovers previously unnoticed design flaws in optimized protocols for the $[[5,1,3]]$ code.
    
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

% Quantum computing promises asymptotic advantages over classical computation. However, realizing these advantages requires protection against noise. Physical qubits are subject to frequent faults from decoherence and imperfect gates.

Quantum computing promises asymptotic advantages over classical computation, but realizing these advantages requires reliable computation with unreliable physical devices. Physical qubits are subject to \emph{faults} caused by decoherence, imperfect gates, faulty measurements, and imperfect state preparation. \emph{Quantum error-correcting codes} (QECCs) address this problem by encoding logical information into multiple physical qubits. A code of distance $d$ can correct up to $t=\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor$ data errors under ideal error correction. In practice, however, the error-correction circuitry is itself faulty. A single fault inside a \emph{syndrome-extraction circuit} may propagate to multiple data qubits, creating correlated errors that exceed the code's correction capability and may cause a logical failure.

% A fault is a circuit-level malfunction, such as a faulty gate or measurement operation. Under the Pauli circuit-level noise model considered in this work, each fault introduces a Pauli error that propagates through the circuit. An error denotes the resulting Pauli operator acting on the data qubits. A protocol is fault-tolerant if, for any set of at most 
% $t$ faults, the induced data error remains correctable for the underlying quantum code.
% In practice, faults may occur during the error-correction procedure. A single internal fault can propagate through the circuit, producing correlated data errors that exceed the code's correction capability and lead to logical failure.

% To address the challenge of fault-tolerant error correction (FTEC), schemes such as those proposed by Shor \cite{Shor96}, Steane \cite{Steane97}, and Knill \cite{Knill05} were developed. While these approaches are conceptually elegant, they require substantial ancillary resources and multiple rounds of verification, leading to considerable overhead for near-term architectures. 

\emph{Fault-tolerant error correction} (FTEC) was developed to address this difficulty. Classical schemes due to Shor~\cite{Shor96}, Steane~\cite{Steane97}, and Knill~\cite{Knill05} achieve fault tolerance by using carefully prepared ancillary states, additional verification circuits, or repeated syndrome extraction. These methods provide strong protection, but they require substantial ancillary resources and circuit depth. This overhead is a major obstacle for near-term fault-tolerant architectures.

% Flagged syndrome extraction protocols offer a resource-efficient alternative. Instead of using encoded states, these protocols instrument the circuit with unencoded \emph{flag qubits}. These flags act as runtime monitors: they signal when a dangerous internal fault has occurred. The control logic then uses this signal to adapt the recovery strategy.

% Flagged syndrome extraction protocols were pioneered by Chao and Reichardt~\cite{Chao18} for a family of distance-3 codes. This approach was subsequently generalized to codes of arbitrary distance by Chamberland and Beverland~\cite{Chamberland18}. More recent research has focused on optimizing these protocols to reduce resource overhead: Reichardt~\cite{reichardt2021fault}, Liou and Lai~\cite{LL23,Liou25}, and Du et al.~\cite{Du24} introduced parallelization and flag-sharing techniques to minimize circuit depth and qubit count. Other approaches, such as low-depth adaptive protocols~\cite{low-depth}, further optimize execution by dynamically selecting specific stabilizers to measure based on intermediate outcomes.

\emph{Flagged syndrome extraction protocols} offer a more resource-efficient alternative. Instead of using large encoded ancilla states, these protocols add a small number of unencoded \emph{flag qubits} to syndrome-extraction circuits. A flag qubit acts as a monitor for dangerous fault propagation: when an internal fault may have produced a high-weight data error, the flag outcome provides additional classical information that the decoder can use to choose a safe recovery. This approach was introduced by Chao and Reichardt~\cite{Chao18} for distance-3 codes and later generalized to arbitrary-distance codes by Chamberland and Beverland~\cite{Chamberland18}. Subsequent work has optimized flagged protocols through parallelization, flag sharing, and adaptive low-depth control flow~\cite{reichardt2021fault,LL23,Liou25,Du24,low-depth}.


% However, designing these protocols is essentially a complex synthesis problem. The correctness depends on the precise interplay of gate ordering, qubit reuse, and measurement scheduling. A subtle design error can create a specific fault path where distinct, non-degenerate errors produce identical measurement outcomes. This causes the error correction to apply an incorrect recovery operation.

Despite their resource advantages, flagged protocols are difficult to design and verify. Their correctness depends on a precise interaction among gate ordering, qubit reuse, measurement scheduling, \emph{classical control flow}, and decoding. A subtle change in the circuit may create two different fault events that produce the same observed measurement outcomes while leaving different \emph{residual errors} on the data qubits. If these residual errors require different corrections, no decoder can safely distinguish the two cases from the available measurement information.

We verify a specific \textbf{correctness property}: for any set of up to $t$ faults, all possible data errors consistent with the observed measurement outcomes must be effectively equivalent (degenerate). We encode the circuit behavior, fault models, and error propagation as Boolean constraints. Using this approach, we automatically verify several proposed protocols and identify subtle design flaws in optimized constructions, finding that the protocols proposed in \cite{Liou25} and  \cite{low-depth} contain a design flaw for the $[[5,1,3]]$ code. \bw{more about finding bug}

This observation exposes a central verification problem: to prove that a flagged protocol is fault-tolerant, one must reason not only about fault propagation in the circuit, but also about whether the measurement information is sufficient for a decoder to recover correctly. A direct approach would construct a decoder and verify its behavior on all relevant fault events. This is undesirable for two reasons. First, decoder construction can obscure the underlying reason why a protocol is correct or incorrect. Second, when a protocol fails, a decoder-dependent verification result may not clearly reveal whether the failure is caused by the decoder or by insufficient information produced by the protocol itself.

In this work, we take a different approach. Rather than verifying a particular decoder, we verify whether a correct decoder exists. Our key result is a \emph{decoder-existence characterization} for flagged syndrome extraction protocols. We show that a fault-tolerant \emph{lookup-table decoder} exists if and only if all fault events consistent with the same \emph{measured generalized syndrome} produce \emph{degenerate residual errors}. Equivalently, every set of residual errors indistinguishable by the protocol's measurement outcomes must belong to a single \emph{stabilizer equivalence class}. If this property holds, any representative of the class can be used as a correction. If it fails, then two non-degenerate residual errors are observationally indistinguishable, and no decoder can correct both.

This characterization turns decoder verification into a \emph{distinguishability problem} over residual errors. It also gives a practical way to find bugs. A counterexample consists of two fault events that produce the same measured generalized syndrome but leave non-degenerate residual data errors. Such a counterexample directly explains why the protocol does not provide enough information for safe recovery.

Based on this characterization, we develop a \emph{SAT-based formal verification framework} for flagged syndrome extraction protocols. Our framework symbolically tracks \emph{Pauli error propagation} through Clifford circuits, models circuit-level Pauli faults, records syndrome and flag measurement outcomes, and encodes the protocol's classical control flow. For each execution path, we ask the SAT solver to search for a pair of indistinguishable fault events whose residual errors are non-degenerate. Unsatisfiability proves that the path satisfies the distinguishability condition; satisfiability yields a concrete fault-level counterexample.

We apply our framework to representative flagged syndrome extraction protocols from the literature. Our tool validates existing constructions and identifies two independent design flaws in optimized protocols for the $[[5,1,3]]$ code: one in protocols based on the optimized constructions of Liou and Lai~\cite{Liou25}, and another in the low-depth adaptive protocols of Bhatnagar et al.~\cite{low-depth}. These examples show that the proposed distinguishability criterion is not only theoretically precise, but also effective at detecting subtle protocol-level errors.

\paragraph{\textbf{Contributions.}}
This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Problem formulation.} We identify decoder existence as the central verification target for flagged syndrome extraction protocols, separating protocol correctness from the construction of a particular decoder.

\item \textbf{Theorem.} We prove a necessary and sufficient characterization: a fault-tolerant lookup-table decoder exists exactly when all fault events with the same measured generalized syndrome produce degenerate residual errors.

\item \textbf{Method.} We develop a SAT-based verification framework that checks this distinguishability condition by symbolic Pauli fault propagation and path-sensitive protocol analysis.

\item \textbf{Case studies.} We evaluate the framework on representative flagged protocols from the literature. The analysis validates existing constructions and uncovers two independent design flaws in optimized $[[5,1,3]]$ protocols: one in protocols based on the optimized constructions of Liou and Lai~\cite{Liou25}, and another in the low-depth adaptive protocols of Bhatnagar et al.~\cite{low-depth}.

\end{itemize}

\section{Preliminaries}
This section fixes the notation and semantic conventions used throughout the paper. We first recall the binary representation of Pauli operators, then review the stabilizer-code notions needed to state our decoder-existence characterization: syndromes, degeneracy, and logical equivalence. We also summarize the measurement facts used to check that the measurement qubits disentangle from the data block. Finally, we define the symbolic Pauli propagation semantics and the circuit-level fault model used by our verifier.
\subsection{Pauli operators and binary representation}
The single-qubit Pauli group is generated by $I$, $X$, $Y$, and $Z$, together with global phases in ${\pm 1,\pm i}$. In this paper, we quotient out global phase and track only the Pauli component. This is sound for our verification problem because global phases do not affect syndromes, measurement outcomes, or logical equivalence. Thus, two Pauli operators that differ only by a global phase are represented by the same value.

We use the standard \emph{binary representation} of Pauli operators:
\[
I \mapsto (0,0), \qquad
X \mapsto (1,0), \qquad
Z \mapsto (0,1), \qquad
Y \mapsto (1,1).
\]
The first bit records the presence of an $X$ component and the second bit records the presence of a $Z$ component. Under this representation, multiplication of Pauli operators corresponds to bitwise XOR:
\[
(1,0)\oplus(0,1)=(1,1),
\]
which represents the identity $XZ=Y$ modulo global phase. Here, $\oplus$ denotes addition modulo two, or exclusive-or on Boolean values. The commutation relation between two single-qubit Pauli operators represented by $(x_1,z_1)$ and $(x_2,z_2)$ is determined by
\[
(x_1 \land z_2) \oplus (z_1 \land x_2).
\]
The value is $0$ if the two operators commute and $1$ if they anti-commute.

An $n$-qubit \emph{Pauli string} is a tensor product $P=P_1\otimes\cdots\otimes P_n$, where each $P_i$ is a single-qubit Pauli operator acting on the $i$-th qubit. The tensor product combines these single-qubit operators into an operator on the full $n$-qubit system. For example, $X\otimes Z\otimes I$ applies $X$ to the first qubit, $Z$ to the second qubit, and $I$ to the third qubit. We write the binary representation of the $i$-th component as $P_i=(P_{i,x},P_{i,z})$.
The \emph{weight} of a Pauli string, denoted $\mathrm{wt}(P)$, is the number of qubits whose component is not the identity: $\mathrm{wt}(P)=
\left|\{i \mid (P_{i,x},P_{i,z})\neq(0,0)\}\right|$.

\subsection{Stabilizer codes, syndromes, and degeneracy}

An $[[n,k,d]]$ \emph{stabilizer code} encodes $k$ logical qubits into $n$ physical qubits and has distance $d$. The code is described by a collection of mutually commuting Pauli strings $G_1,\ldots,G_{n-k}$,
called \emph{stabilizer generators}. These generators define the code space: $\ket{\psi}$ is valid iff $G_i\ket{\psi}=\ket{\psi}$ for all $i$. The stabilizer generators can be multiplied together to form additional Pauli strings that also fix every code state. We write
$\mathcal{S}=\langle G_1,\ldots,G_{n-k}\rangle$
for the set of all such products, including the empty product $I$. This set is called the \emph{stabilizer group}.
We also fix a set of \emph{logical Pauli operators}
$\mathcal{L}
=
\{\bar X_1,\ldots,\bar X_k,\bar Z_1,\ldots,\bar Z_k\}$.
Logical Pauli operators commute with all stabilizers but are not themselves stabilizers. They represent operations on the encoded logical qubits.

For a Pauli error $E$, the \emph{syndrome} of $E$ with respect to a stabilizer generator $G_i$ is a bit indicating whether $E$ commutes with $G_i$. The bit is $0$ if $E$ commutes with $G_i$ and $1$ if $E$ anti-commutes with $G_i$. The \emph{full syndrome} is the vector of syndrome bits with respect to all stabilizer generators.
Using the binary representation from the previous subsection, the syndrome bit of $E$ with respect to a Pauli string $P$ is
$$\mathrm{syn}_P(E)
=
\bigoplus_{j=1}^n
\bigl(
(E_{j,x}\land P_{j,z})
\oplus
(E_{j,z}\land P_{j,x})
\bigr).$$ Thus, the full syndrome is
$\mathrm{syn}(E)
=
\bigl(
\mathrm{syn}_{G_1}(E),
\ldots,
\mathrm{syn}_{G_{n-k}}(E)
\bigr)$. Syndromes are linear: $$\mathrm{syn}(E_1E_2)
=
\mathrm{syn}(E_1)
\oplus
\mathrm{syn}(E_2).$$ This follows directly from the fact that Pauli multiplication is XOR in the binary representation.

Two Pauli errors $E_1$ and $E_2$ are \emph{degenerate}, written $E_1\sim E_2$, if they differ by a stabilizer, i.e., $E_1E_2\in\mathcal{S}$ up to global phase.
Degenerate errors act identically on encoded states and therefore require the same correction. For an error $E$, its stabilizer equivalence class is
$$[E]_{\mathcal{S}}
=
\{ES \mid S\in\mathcal{S}\}.$$
We define the \emph{minimum weight} of $E$ modulo stabilizer degeneracy as
$\mathrm{wt}_{\min}(E)
=
\min_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\mathrm{wt}(ES)$.

Under a Clifford unitary $U$, the state's evolution is tracked by conjugating the generators: $g_i \to U g_i U^\dagger$.

\subsection{Stabilizer measurements and deterministic outcomes}

We use a basic fact from the stabilizer formalism: measuring a Pauli string is deterministic when the string, up to sign, belongs to the current stabilizer group. Let $\mathcal{S}$ be the current stabilizer group and let $M$ be the Pauli string being measured. If $M\in\mathcal{S}$ or $-M\in\mathcal{S}$, the outcome is deterministic; otherwise, the outcome is random and the measurement changes the stabilizer state.
\bw{M only has to commute with all stabilizer in S}


This distinction matters for syndrome extraction. A \emph{syndrome-extraction circuit} is a circuit that couples measurement qubits to the data block and then measures those qubits to obtain stabilizer syndrome bits.
During such a circuit, a measurement qubit may become temporarily entangled with the data block. At the end of a well-formed extraction circuit, the measurement qubit should be disentangled from the data block, and its measurement result should be determined only by the intended syndrome of the data error.

Concretely, suppose a syndrome-extraction circuit is designed to extract the syndrome bit for a stabilizer generator $P$, and let $E$ be the propagated data \bw{may be the error dont prpagate?} error. The intended measurement result is the ideal syndrome bit $\mathrm{syn}_P(E)$, as defined above. If the extraction circuit is well formed, the measurement qubit disentangles from the data block at the end of the circuit, and its measurement outcome is determined by this syndrome bit.

The updated stabilizer group becomes $\mathcal{S}' = \langle (-1)^s Z \rangle$, which perfectly describes the new post-measurement state.
% \end{itemize}

For a circuit with $N$ qubits, we write the symbolic Pauli state as $Q=Q_1\cdots Q_N$.

\subsection{Circuit-level fault model}

We use a Pauli circuit-level fault model. A \emph{fault} is a malfunction at a circuit location, and its effect is modeled as an inserted Pauli error. For a one-qubit operation, a fault may insert an error from $\{X,Y,Z\}$ on the affected qubit. For a two-qubit operation on qubits $i$ and $j$, a fault may insert an error $P_i\otimes P_j$, where $P_i,P_j\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}$ and not both are $I$.

Measurement and preparation faults are modeled in the same Pauli-error style. A measurement fault is represented as a Pauli error immediately before the measurement; for example, an $X$ error before a $Z$-basis measurement flips the recorded measurement bit. Preparation faults are modeled as Pauli errors immediately after preparation. Regardless of whether the qubit is initialized in $\ket{0}$ or $\ket{+}$, a preparation fault may insert any non-identity Pauli error from $\{X,Y,Z\}$ on the prepared qubit. Idle faults are also modeled as single-qubit Pauli errors on idle qubits.

For the syndrome-extraction circuits considered in this paper, every preparation, measurement, idle, and one-qubit fault can be represented, for verification purposes, as a Pauli error associated with a neighboring two-qubit gate location. Fig.~\ref{equivalence_of_fault} illustrates the basic equivalence: a single-qubit Pauli error between two Clifford gates can be shifted into either adjacent two-qubit gate fault, and a measurement fault can be represented as a Pauli error immediately before the measurement and absorbed into the preceding two-qubit gate fault. Thus, our implementation introduces fault variables only at two-qubit gate locations, while preserving the behavior of the circuit-level fault model.
The verification problems considered in this paper are parameterized by a fault bound $t$. We consider all fault events consisting of at most $t$ faulty locations, with each faulty location assigned a Pauli error according to the model above.

\section{Flagged Syndrome Extraction and Decoder Existence}\label{sec:flagged-decoder-existence}
This section formalizes the objects needed to state our decoder-existence characterization. We first define syndrome-extraction protocols and their measurement records. We then extend this model to flagged protocols, define residual error sets indexed by measured generalized syndromes, and prove that decoder existence is equivalent to residual-error distinguishability.
\subsection{Syndrome-extraction protocols}
\label{subsec:syndrome-extraction-protocols}
Let $\mathcal{Q}$ be an $[[n,k,d]]$ stabilizer code with stabilizer generators $G_1,\ldots,G_{n-k}$. A \emph{syndrome-extraction circuit} for a stabilizer generator $P$ is a circuit that couples the data block to one or more measurement qubits and then measures those qubits to extract the syndrome bit $\mathrm{syn}_P(E)$ of a data error $E$. The circuit should not directly measure the data qubits. Instead, it transfers the relevant stabilizer information to the measurement qubits and leaves the data block disentangled from them at the end of the circuit.

We distinguish between the \emph{ideal syndrome} and the \emph{measured syndrome}. The ideal syndrome of a data error $E$ is the value computed algebraically from its commutation relation with the stabilizer generators, namely
$\mathrm{syn}(E)=(\mathrm{syn}_{G_1}(E),\ldots,\mathrm{syn}_{G_{n-k}}(E))$.
The measured syndrome is the classical bit string obtained by executing the syndrome-extraction circuits and reading out the measurement qubits. 
A syndrome-extraction circuit is \emph{fault-free valid} if, in the fault-free case with no input error, every measurement outcome used by the protocol is deterministic and agrees with the fault-free value expected by the protocol, and the induced operation on the data block is logically equivalent to the identity.
In addition, the circuit is \emph{syndrome-correct} for a stabilizer $P$ if, in the absence of circuit faults, its deterministic syndrome outcome equals $\mathrm{syn}_P(E)$ for every input Pauli data error $E$.

A \emph{full syndrome-extraction round} consists of syndrome-extraction circuits for all stabilizer generators $G_1,\ldots,G_{n-k}$. Its ideal output on a data error $E$ is the full syndrome $\mathrm{syn}(E)$. A syndrome-extraction protocol may execute one or more such circuits or rounds, possibly depending on previous measurement outcomes. The classical measurement record produced by the protocol is the information available to a decoder.

A \emph{lookup-table decoder} maps each possible measurement record to a Pauli correction on the data qubits. If the residual data error after the protocol is $E$ and the decoder applies correction $C$, then the post-correction residual error is $CE$. The correction succeeds without changing the encoded logical state precisely when $CE\in\mathcal{S}$, where $\mathcal{S}$ is the stabilizer group of the code. Thus, from the perspective of decoder existence, what matters is not which decoder is constructed, but whether the measurement record partitions possible residual errors into stabilizer-equivalent classes.

Flagged syndrome extraction augments syndrome-extraction circuits with additional measurement qubits, called \emph{flag qubits}, that are used to detect dangerous fault propagation. Intuitively, a flag qubit is not used to extract a stabilizer syndrome bit. Instead, it records whether a fault may have propagated into a data error whose weight is too large to be safely inferred from the syndrome alone.

Following the flag-FTEC framework of Chamberland and Beverland~\cite{Chamberland18}, we use the following condition to formalize the role of flag qubits. We say that a flagged syndrome-extraction circuit satisfies the \emph{flag condition} up to fault bound $t$ if, for every $w\le t$, no $w$-fault event can both leave all flag outcomes equal to $0$ and produce a residual data error $E$ with $\mathrm{wt}_{\min}(E)>w$. Equivalently, whenever a $w$-fault event produces a residual data error whose minimum weight modulo stabilizer degeneracy  \bw{modulo stabilizer?} exceeds $w$, at least one flag qubit must output $1$. This condition ensures that high-weight errors produced by few faults are not silently hidden from the classical control logic.

A \emph{flagged syndrome-extraction circuit} is a syndrome-extraction circuit equipped with flag qubits. It contains data qubits, syndrome-extraction qubits, and flag qubits. The syndrome-extraction qubits are measured to obtain stabilizer syndrome bits, while the flag qubits are measured to indicate potentially dangerous fault propagation. In the absence of circuit faults, a flagged syndrome-extraction circuit is required to satisfy the same validity and syndrome-correctness conditions as an unflagged syndrome extraction circuit. In particular, its protocol-visible measurement outcomes must be deterministic in the fault-free no-input-error case, it must induce no unintended logical operation on the data block, and its syndrome-extraction outcomes must agree with the corresponding ideal syndrome bits for arbitrary input Pauli data errors.

A \emph{flagged syndrome-extraction protocol} consists of a collection of flagged and unflagged syndrome-extraction circuits together with a classical control flow. The control flow determines which circuit is executed next based on the syndrome and flag outcomes observed so far, and it determines when the protocol terminates. Thus, an execution of the protocol follows a path through a classical decision tree. Each path is associated with a sequence of extraction circuits and a set of branch conditions over their measurement outcomes.

The classical information available at termination is the \emph{measured generalized syndrome} (mgs). It consists of all syndrome-extraction outcomes and flag outcomes observed along the executed path. The mgs therefore records not only the final measured syndrome bits, but also the intermediate flag and syndrome information that determined the protocol branch. A lookup-table decoder for a flagged protocol maps each terminating mgs to a Pauli correction on the data qubits.

Fig.~\ref{fig:five-qubit-flagged-protocol} illustrates this structure for a flagged protocol on the $[[5,1,3]]$ code. Each internal node is a syndrome-extraction circuit, and each outgoing edge is guarded by a condition on the syndrome and flag outcomes. A terminating path determines a measured generalized syndrome consisting of all outcomes collected along that path.

\subsection{Residual error sets}
We now associate each terminating measured generalized syndrome with the set of residual data errors that must be handled by the corresponding recovery action. Fix a fault bound $t$. For a terminating measured generalized syndrome $m$, let $\mathcal{E}_m$ denote the set of residual Pauli errors on the data block that remain possible after observing $m$, under fault events of size at most $t$. We call $\mathcal{E}_m$ the \emph{residual error set} for $m$.

This definition is semantic. An error $E$ belongs to $\mathcal{E}_m$ if there exists an execution of the protocol, with at most $t$ circuit-level faults, that terminates with measured generalized syndrome $m$ and leaves residual data error $E$, subject to the case-specific compatibility conditions described below. The decoder does not observe which fault event occurred. It only observes $m$. Therefore, all errors in $\mathcal{E}_m$ must be handled by the same lookup-table entry.

Following the protocol structure of Chamberland and Beverland~\cite{Chamberland18}, the protocols considered in this paper organize syndrome extraction into \emph{full-syndrome attempts}. A full-syndrome attempt is an ordered sequence of syndrome-extraction circuits intended to measure all stabilizer generators. The attempt may be cut short: if one extraction circuit produces a branch-triggering outcome, such as a raised flag or a nonzero measured syndrome, the protocol stops the remaining circuits in the current attempt. It then either terminates, if a termination condition is satisfied, or proceeds to the next full-syndrome attempt. If no such outcome occurs and all stabilizer generators are measured, the attempt is completed and produces one full measured syndrome.

The control flow maintains a protocol-level counter $w$ of detected fault indicators. This counter is computed from the measurement record rather than from the actual fault locations. It is incremented by one whenever a full-syndrome attempt is interrupted by a raised flag, and also when two completed full-syndrome attempts selected for comparison produce different full measured syndromes. These comparisons are non-overlapping: once a disagreement between $F_i$ and $F_{i+1}$ has been counted, the next comparison starts at $F_{i+2}$.

Although the value of $w$ and the termination decision are determined dynamically during execution, the verifier treats the protocol statically by unfolding this measurement-dependent control flow into execution paths. Each edge is labeled by a Boolean condition over syndrome and flag outcomes, and each root-to-leaf path represents one terminating execution. Fig.~\ref{fig:five-qubit-flagged-protocol} shows an example of such a static unfolding.

For the protocols considered in this paper, terminating executions fall into two cases. The two cases differ in what information is available for defining compatibility with $m$. In the distinguishability case, the protocol does not obtain a repeated full syndrome that can be treated as the ideal syndrome. Thus, compatibility with $m$ only means that the execution produces the same measured generalized syndrome. In the repeated-syndrome case, the protocol does obtain a common repeated full syndrome, and this value is treated as the ideal syndrome of the residual error. This imposes an additional ideal-syndrome restriction on $\mathcal{E}_m$.

In the \emph{distinguishability case} \bw{?}, the protocol has observed $t$ flag interruptions. A flag interruption occurs when a full-syndrome attempt is cut short because one of its flagged extraction circuits raises a flag. Once $t$ such interruptions have been observed, the protocol follows the remaining circuits on that terminating branch and invokes the decoder without requiring additional consecutive agreeing full measured syndromes. For the resulting measured generalized syndrome $m$, the residual error set $\mathcal{E}_m$ contains every residual data error that can arise from any fault event of size at most $t$ whose execution produces the same $m$. Correctness of this case is determined by whether all errors in $\mathcal{E}_m$ are mutually degenerate.

In the \emph{repeated-syndrome case}, the detected-fault counter has value $w<t$, and the protocol obtains $t-w+1$ consecutive completed full-syndrome attempts with the same full measured syndrome and with no additional flag raised. Let $s_{\mathrm{rep}}$ \bw{maybe $syn_{\mathrm{rep}}$ } denote this common full measured syndrome. The protocol treats $s_{\mathrm{rep}}$ as the ideal syndrome of the residual data error. Accordingly, the residual error set is restricted in this case: an error $E$ belongs to $\mathcal{E}_m$ only if its execution produces the measured generalized syndrome $m$ and $\mathrm{syn}(E)=s{\mathrm{rep}}$. When $w=0$ and $s_{\mathrm{rep}}$ is the zero syndrome, the protocol has observed neither a nonzero syndrome nor a raised flag; operationally, it may terminate without invoking the decoder, which is equivalent to using the identity correction.

The purpose of defining $\mathcal{E}_m$ is to isolate the decoder-existence question. A lookup-table decoder must choose one correction for each terminating measured generalized syndrome $m$. The next subsection shows that such a decoder exists exactly when each residual error set $\mathcal{E}_m$ can be corrected by a single Pauli correction, equivalently, when all errors in $\mathcal{E}_m$ are mutually degenerate.

\subsection{Decoder existence via residual-error distinguishability}

We now characterize when a lookup-table decoder exists for the residual error sets defined above. The decoder receives only the measured generalized syndrome $m$, and must therefore choose a single correction for all residual errors in $\mathcal{E}_m$. The key question is whether such a single correction can work for every residual error that remains possible after observing $m$.

For a fixed terminating measured generalized syndrome $m$, we say that $\mathcal{E}_m$ is \emph{degeneracy-consistent} if all of its elements belong to the same stabilizer equivalence class:
\[
  \forall E_1,E_2\in\mathcal{E}_m,\qquad E_1E_2\in\mathcal{S}.
\]
Equivalently, any two residual errors consistent with the same measured generalized syndrome differ only by a stabilizer, up to global phase.

\begin{lemma}[Decoder existence for a fixed measured generalized syndrome]
Let $m$ be a terminating measured generalized syndrome and let $\mathcal{E}_m$ be its residual error set. There exists a Pauli correction $C_m$ such that
\[
  \forall E\in\mathcal{E}_m,\qquad C_mE\in\mathcal{S}
\]
if and only if $\mathcal{E}_m$ is degeneracy-consistent.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$(\Rightarrow)$ Suppose such a correction $C_m$ exists. For any $E_1,E_2\in\mathcal{E}_m$, we have $C_mE_1\in\mathcal{S}$ and $C_mE_2\in\mathcal{S}$. Since $\mathcal{S}$ is closed under multiplication, their product is also in $\mathcal{S}$. Up to global phase,
\[
  (C_mE_1)(C_mE_2)=E_1E_2.
\]
Hence $E_1E_2\in\mathcal{S}$, so $\mathcal{E}_m$ is degeneracy-consistent.

$(\Leftarrow)$ Suppose $\mathcal{E}_m$ is degeneracy-consistent. Choose any representative $E_0\in\mathcal{E}_m$ and set $C_m=E_0$. Then for every $E\in\mathcal{E}_m$, degeneracy consistency gives $E_0E\in\mathcal{S}$. Hence $C_mE\in\mathcal{S}$, so $C_m$ corrects every residual error in $\mathcal{E}_m$ without changing the encoded logical state.
\end{proof}

The lemma gives a local condition for one measured generalized syndrome. Applying it to every terminating measured generalized syndrome yields the decoder-existence characterization.

\begin{theorem}[Decoder existence via residual-error distinguishability]\label{thm:decoder}
A lookup-table decoder exists for all terminating executions of the flagged syndrome-extraction protocol if and only if every residual error set $\mathcal{E}_m$ is degeneracy-consistent:
\[
  \forall m,\ \forall E_1,E_2\in\mathcal{E}_m,\qquad E_1E_2\in\mathcal{S}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
A lookup-table decoder chooses one correction $C_m$ for each terminating measured generalized syndrome $m$. By the preceding lemma, such a correction exists for a fixed $m$ exactly when $\mathcal{E}_m$ is degeneracy-consistent. Therefore, a lookup-table decoder exists for all terminating executions exactly when this condition holds for every residual error set $\mathcal{E}_m$.
\end{proof}

This theorem turns decoder existence into a distinguishability condition. If two fault events produce the same measured generalized syndrome but leave residual errors $E_1$ and $E_2$ with $E_1E_2\notin\mathcal{S}$, then no lookup-table decoder can correct both cases using the same entry for $m$. Conversely, if no such pair exists, the decoder can choose any representative residual error from each $\mathcal{E}_m$ as the correction for $m$.

We now connect this decoder-existence characterization to fault-tolerant error correction. The preceding theorem ensures that the protocol has enough measurement information to select a logically correct recovery. The remaining requirement in the standard $t$-FTEC definition is the residual-weight condition: after an execution with sufficiently few input errors and circuit faults, the output must not contain an uncorrectable residual error. For the Chamberland--Beverland-style protocols considered here, this weight condition is supplied by the terminating cases described above. In the distinguishability case, the residual errors are handled directly by the decoder entry for $m$. In the repeated-syndrome case, the common repeated full syndrome fixes the ideal syndrome of the residual error, while the absence of additional flags ensures that the remaining unflagged faults do not produce a dangerous high-weight residual error.

The following theorem assumes that the circuit-level checks described below hold: extraction circuits are \emph{fault-free valid} (defined in~\ref{subsec:syndrome-extraction-protocols}), their syndrome-extraction outcomes are \emph{syndrome-correct} (defined in~\ref{subsec:syndrome-extraction-protocols}) for arbitrary input Pauli data errors in the absence of circuit faults, and flagged circuits satisfy the \emph{flag condition} (defined in~\ref{subsec:flagged-protocols}) up to the fault bound $t$.
\begin{theorem}[Fault-tolerant recovery]\label{thm:fault-tolerant-recovery}
Consider a Chamberland--Beverland-style flagged syndrome-extraction protocol with fault bound $t$ satisfying the circuit-level conditions above. If every residual error set $\mathcal{E}_m$ is degeneracy-consistent, then there exists a lookup-table decoder such that the protocol together with this decoder is $t$-fault-tolerant.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By the decoder-existence theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:decoder}), degeneracy consistency of every $\mathcal{E}_m$ gives a lookup-table decoder assigning a correction $C_m$ to each terminating measured generalized syndrome $m$ such that $C_mE\in\mathcal{S}$ for every $E\in\mathcal{E}_m$. Thus, for every fault event covered by the fault bound, the decoder preserves the encoded logical state.

The circuit-level assumptions provide the remaining ingredients required for $t$-FTEC. Fault-free validity ensures that the fault-free extraction circuits have deterministic measurement outcomes and no unintended logical action on the data block. Syndrome correctness ensures that measured syndrome information agrees with the ideal syndrome of the propagated data error in the absence of circuit faults. The flag condition ensures that unflagged faults cannot silently produce residual data errors whose minimum weight exceeds the corresponding fault budget. Together with the Chamberland--Beverland termination structure, these properties give the residual-weight guarantee required by the $t$-FTEC definition. \bw{definition?}

Therefore, the protocol together with the lookup-table decoder satisfies both requirements of $t$-fault-tolerant error correction: it preserves the encoded logical state and maintains the required residual-weight bound.
\end{proof}

\section{SAT-Based Verification}
\label{sec:sat-verification}


This section presents our verification procedure for flagged syndrome-extraction protocols. The goal is not to synthesize or validate a particular decoder. Instead, following the characterization in Section~\ref{sec:flagged-decoder-existence}, the verifier checks whether a suitable lookup-table decoder exists. The central verification task is therefore to rule out pairs of fault events that produce the same terminating measured generalized syndrome but leave residual data errors that are not stabilizer-equivalent.

The verifier combines fault-free stabilizer simulation with SAT-based symbolic Pauli propagation. The first step uses standard stabilizer-tableau simulation and stabilizer-membership tests to check fault-free circuit validity, namely deterministic measurement outcomes and absence of unintended logical action. The remaining steps encode syndrome correctness, the flag condition, and residual-error distinguishability as satisfiability queries. A satisfying assignment represents a concrete counterexample, such as an incorrect syndrome bit, an unflagged high-weight residual error, or a pair of indistinguishable nondegenerate residual errors. Conversely, unsatisfiability proves that the corresponding bad behavior cannot occur within the specified fault bound.

\subsection{Symbolic execution of protocol paths}
\label{subsec:symbolic-execution-paths}

\pathexample

This subsection introduces the symbolic objects used by the SAT encodings below: terminating paths $\pi$, path-level fault assignments $F$, path conditions $\Phi_\pi(F)$, fault weights $\mathrm{wt}(F)$, residual data errors $E_\pi(F)$, and terminating outcome vectors $o_\pi(F)$.

\paragraph{Terminating paths $\pi$.}
The verifier treats a flagged syndrome-extraction protocol as a measurement-dependent control-flow graph. Its nodes are syndrome-extraction circuits, and its edges are guarded by Boolean conditions over the syndrome and flag-qubit measurement outcomes observed so far. To analyze the protocol statically, the verifier unfolds this control flow into a finite collection of terminating paths. A terminating path $\pi$ determines a sequence of circuits $\mathcal{C}_\pi=(C_1,\ldots,C_r)$, where $C_j$ is the $j$-th extraction circuit executed along that path.


\paragraph{Path-level fault assignments $F$ and fault weight $\mathrm{wt}(F)$.}
In our fault model, fault locations are the two-qubit gates in the circuits. For a terminating path $\pi$, a path-level fault assignment $F$ assigns a two-qubit Pauli operator to each such gate in the circuit sequence $\mathcal{C}_\pi$. Figure~\ref{fig:path-example-circuit} marks one such fault location $\ell$ in the first circuit block $C_1$. The two qubits touched by $\ell$ are data qubit $d_1$ and syndrome-extraction qubit $a$. The fault at $\ell$ is represented by four Boolean variables $(f_{\ell,d_1,x},f_{\ell,d_1,z},f_{\ell,a,x},f_{\ell,a,z})$. The all-zero assignment represents the identity operator and therefore means that $\ell$ is not faulty. We introduce an activity variable $a_\ell$ defined by $a_\ell=f_{\ell,d_1,x}\lor f_{\ell,d_1,z}\lor f_{\ell,a,x}\lor f_{\ell,a,z}$, so $a_\ell=1$ exactly when the Pauli operator assigned to $\ell$ is not the identity.  Constraints on $\mathrm{wt}(F)$, such as $\mathrm{wt}(F)\le t$ and $\mathrm{wt}(F)=w$, are cardinality constraints over the activity variables. In the implementation, these cardinality constraints are translated to Boolean formulas using standard encodings~\cite{sinz2005towards}.

\paragraph{Path conditions $\Phi_\pi(F)$.}
Symbolic execution propagates the Pauli components assigned by $F$ through the circuits along the path. For the local illustration in Figure~\ref{fig:path-example-circuit}, consider only the contribution of the marked fault location $\ell$. If the incoming Pauli error before $\ell$ is the identity on all qubits, then immediately after the fault at $\ell$ the symbolic Pauli components are $d_1=(f_{\ell,d_1,x},f_{\ell,d_1,z})$ and $a=(f_{\ell,a,x},f_{\ell,a,z})$. In the general symbolic semantics, these inserted components are XORed into the current Pauli variables of the corresponding qubits.

The next two-qubit gate acts with $a$ as the control qubit and the flag qubit $f$ as the target. By the $CX$ propagation rule in Figure~\ref{fig:two-qubit-pauli-rules}, after this gate the contribution from $\ell$ gives $a=(f_{\ell,a,x},f_{\ell,a,z})$ and $f=(f_{\ell,a,x},0)$. Thus, the fault variable $f_{\ell,a,x}$ already appears in the flag-qubit Pauli state after one further gate. Continuing this propagation through the rest of the path turns each recorded syndrome or flag-qubit measurement outcome into a Boolean formula over the fault variables in $F$.

These recorded-outcome formulas determine which branch the protocol takes after each circuit. We write $\Phi_\pi(F)$ for the conjunction of all branch conditions along the path. Thus, $\Phi_\pi(F)$ holds exactly when the outcomes produced under $F$ make the protocol follow the terminating path $\pi$.

\paragraph{Symbolic outputs $E_\pi(F)$ and $o_\pi(F)$.}
Symbolic Pauli propagation along the fixed circuit sequence $\mathcal{C}_\pi$ produces two outputs used by the later SAT queries. The first is the residual data error $E_\pi(F)$, a binary Pauli vector on the data block at the end of the path. The second is the terminating outcome vector $o_\pi(F)$, which contains all syndrome and flag-qubit measurement outcomes visible to the decoder at termination. Thus, $o_\pi(F)$ is the measured generalized syndrome produced by this symbolic execution. When $\Phi_\pi(F)$ holds, these outputs correspond to an actual execution that follows path $\pi$.

In the example of Figure~\ref{fig:path-example-flow}, the path is $\pi=(C_1,\ldots,C_6)$. The first circuit $C_1=(XZZXI)_f$ produces the recorded outcomes $R_{XZZXI,1}(F)$ and $R_{\mathrm{flag},1,1}(F)$ used in the first branch condition. The second circuit $C_2=(IXZZX)_f$ produces $R_{IXZZX,2}(F)$ and $R_{\mathrm{flag},2,1}(F)$ used in the second branch condition. Here $R_{P,i}(F)$ denotes the recorded syndrome outcome of the $i$-th extraction of stabilizer $P$, and $R_{\mathrm{flag},i,j}(F)$ denotes the recorded outcome of the $j$-th flag qubit in that extraction. After the full syndrome-extraction block $C_3,\ldots,C_6$, symbolic execution produces the final residual data error $E_\pi(F)$ and the terminating outcome vector $o_\pi(F)$, which collects all recorded outcomes from $C_1,\ldots,C_6$.

The following subsections use $\Phi_\pi(F)$, $\mathrm{wt}(F)$, $E_\pi(F)$, and $o_\pi(F)$ directly in the SAT queries, with the relevant constraints expanded explicitly.


\subsection{Step 4: Residual-error distinguishability}
\label{subsec:residual-error-distinguishability}

The fourth step is the main protocol-level check. It verifies the decoder-existence condition from Section~\ref{sec:flagged-decoder-existence}: for each terminating measured generalized syndrome, all residual data errors consistent with that observation must be stabilizer-equivalent. Assuming the circuit-level checks from Steps~1--3 pass, it remains to rule out the following bad pair: two terminating executions that produce the same terminating outcome vector but leave residual data errors in different stabilizer cosets.

Because the terminating outcome vector records the branch outcomes observed along the executed path, each such vector determines a unique terminating path. Therefore, the verifier checks bad pairs path by path. For each terminating path $\pi$, it asks whether there exist two path-level fault assignments $F_1$ and $F_2$ such that both executions follow $\pi$, both satisfy the fault bound, both produce the same terminating outcome vector, but their residual data errors are not stabilizer-equivalent.

The base bad-pair query is
\[
\begin{aligned}
\exists F_1,F_2.\quad
&
\Phi_\pi(F_1)
\land
\Phi_\pi(F_2)
\\
&\land\ \mathrm{wt}(F_1)\le t
\land
\mathrm{wt}(F_2)\le t
\\
&\land\ o_\pi(F_1)=o_\pi(F_2)
\\
&\land\ E_\pi(F_1)E_\pi(F_2)\notin\mathcal{S}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The first line enforces that both symbolic executions follow the terminating path $\pi$. The second line enforces the fault bound. The third line says that the decoder observes the same measured generalized syndrome in both executions. The last line says that the two residual data errors are not degenerate, so they cannot be corrected by the same lookup-table entry.

This base query is refined according to the termination case of $\pi$. For a distinguishability termination path, no additional constraint is added. For a repeated-syndrome termination path, the terminating outcome vector $o_\pi(F)$ contains the repeated full syndrome observed at termination. The query additionally requires the ideal syndrome of each residual error to equal the repeated full syndrome. Since the bad-pair query also enforces $o_\pi(F_1)=o_\pi(F_2)$, both executions use the same repeated full syndrome.

It remains to encode the stabilizer-nonmembership condition. Let $Q=E_\pi(F_1)E_\pi(F_2)$. In the binary Pauli representation, this product is computed by XORing the two residual-error vectors. The verifier then checks whether $Q$ lies outside the stabilizer group $\mathcal{S}$
Let $G_{\mathcal S}$ be the binary matrix whose rows are the stabilizer generators. We precompute a binary matrix $H_{\mathcal S}$ whose kernel is the span of these rows. Then a binary Pauli vector $Q$ belongs to $\mathcal S$ iff
$H_{\mathcal S}Q=0$.
Therefore, $Q\notin\mathcal S$ is encoded as
\[
  \bigvee_i (H_{\mathcal S}Q)_i = 1 .
\]
This gives a Boolean constraint for the final line of the bad-pair query.

If the bad-pair query is satisfiable, the model gives two fault assignments that lead to the same terminating outcome vector but leave nondegenerate residual data errors. Such a model is a concrete decoder-existence counterexample: no decoder can choose one correction for that outcome vector and correct both executions. If the query is unsatisfiable for every terminating path, then any two residual errors associated with the same measured generalized syndrome are stabilizer-equivalent. Hence, every residual-error set is degeneracy-consistent, and the decoder-existence condition holds.

\subsection{Step 3: Flag-condition checking}
\label{subsec:flag-condition-checking}

The third step checks that each flagged syndrome-extraction circuit satisfies the flag condition. This check is local to a single flagged extraction circuit. Its purpose is to rule out the bad case where \(w\) circuit faults produce no flag but leave a residual data error that is not stabilizer-equivalent to any Pauli error of weight at most \(w\).

Let \(C\) be a flagged syndrome-extraction circuit, and let \(\mathcal{F}_C\) be the set of flag qubits in \(C\). Symbolic execution of \(C\) under a circuit-level fault assignment \(F\) produces a residual data error \(E_C(F)\) on the data block and, for each \(q\in\mathcal{F}_C\), a Boolean formula \(f_q(F)\) for the measured outcome of \(q\). The fault weight \(\mathrm{wt}(F)\) is the number of active fault locations, as defined in Section~\ref{subsec:symbolic-execution-paths}.

For each \(w=1,\ldots,t\), the verifier asks whether the following query is satisfiable:
\[
\exists F.\quad
\mathrm{wt}(F)=w
\land
\bigwedge_{q\in\mathcal{F}_C}\bigl(f_q(F)=0\bigr)
\land
\mathrm{wt}_{\min}\bigl(E_C(F)\bigr)>w .
\]
The first conjunct fixes the number of circuit faults. The second conjunct requires that no flag qubit is raised. The last conjunct says that the residual data error is too large even after accounting for stabilizer degeneracy.

Here
\[
\mathrm{wt}_{\min}(E)
=
\min_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\mathrm{wt}(ES)
\]
denotes the minimum weight among all Pauli errors stabilizer-equivalent to \(E\). The verifier encodes the condition \(\mathrm{wt}_{\min}(E_C(F))>w\) by explicitly ranging over stabilizer representatives. Let \(\mathcal{S}=\langle G_1,\ldots,G_{n-k}\rangle\). For Boolean coefficients \(c_{G_1},\ldots,c_{G_{n-k}}\), write
\[
S(c)=G_1^{c_{G_1}}\cdots G_{n-k}^{c_{G_{n-k}}}.
\]
For each choice of \(c\),
the representative \(E_C(F)S(c)\)
is obtained by XORing the binary Pauli vectors
of \(E_C(F)\) and \(S(c)\).
Thus,
\[
\bigwedge_{c\in\{0,1\}^{n-k}}
\mathrm{wt}(E_C(F)S(c))>w .
\]
Each weight constraint is translated to CNF using
the same cardinality encoding used for fault-weight constraints.


If the query is satisfiable for some \(w\), the model gives a concrete \(w\)-fault pattern that raises no flag but leaves a residual data error not equivalent to any Pauli error of weight at most \(w\). The circuit then violates the flag condition. If the query is unsatisfiable for every \(w=1,\ldots,t\), then every unflagged \(w\)-fault residual error is stabilizer-equivalent to an error of weight at most \(w\), and the circuit passes the flag-condition check.

\subsection{Step 2: Syndrome correctness}

The second step checks that each syndrome-extraction circuit reports the intended stabilizer syndrome. This check is fault-free, but unlike Step~1 it allows an arbitrary input Pauli error on the data qubits.

Let \(C\) be a syndrome-extraction circuit for a stabilizer \(P\). The verifier introduces a symbolic input Pauli error \(E\) on the data qubits and propagates it through the fault-free circuit. Symbolic execution produces a Boolean formula \(M_P(E)\) for the recorded measurement bit.

The ideal syndrome bit \(\mathrm{syn}_P(E)\) is the commutation bit between \(E\) and \(P\). That is, \(\mathrm{syn}_P(E)=1\) exactly when \(E\) anticommutes with \(P\), and \(\mathrm{syn}_P(E)=0\) exactly when \(E\) commutes with \(P\). The circuit is syndrome-correct if the reported measurement bit agrees with the ideal syndrome bit for every input Pauli error.

The verifier checks this universal condition by asking the SAT solver for a counterexample:
\[
  \exists E.\quad
  M_P(E)\ne \mathrm{syn}_P(E).
\]
If the query is satisfiable, the model gives an input Pauli error for which the circuit reports an incorrect syndrome bit. If the query is unsatisfiable, then the reported measurement bit agrees with the ideal syndrome bit for all input Pauli errors.

\subsection{Step 1: Fault-free circuit validity}
\label{subsec:fault-free-circuit-validity}

Step~1 is a standard fault-free stabilizer check. Unlike Steps~2--4, it is not encoded as a SAT query: there are no symbolic faults and no search over input errors. We therefore describe it only briefly. The check uses standard stabilizer-tableau simulation and stabilizer-membership tests to verify that each syndrome-extraction circuit is fault-free valid in the sense of Section~\ref{sec:flagged-decoder-existence}. In the fault-free case with no input data error, this means that (1) every measurement outcome is deterministic and agrees with the value expected by the protocol, and (2) the induced operation on the data qubits is logically equivalent to the identity.

The check is performed by gate-by-gate stabilizer simulation. Let $\mathcal{S}=\langle G_1,\ldots,G_{n-k}\rangle$ be the stabilizer group of the data qubits. Those stabilizers are extended with identity on the measurement qubits, and measurement-qubit preparation stabilizers are extended with identity on the data qubits and all other measurement qubits. The verifier initializes the extended stabilizer group $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{ext}}=\left\langle G_1,\ldots,G_{n-k}, P_1,\ldots,P_r \right\rangle$. Here $P_1,\ldots,P_r$ fix the initial states of the measurement qubits. An ancilla prepared in $\ket{0}$ contributes its $Z$ stabilizer, while an ancilla prepared in $\ket{+}$ contributes its $X$ stabilizer.

In our circuits, each measurement qubit is measured in the basis in which it was prepared. Hence, the verifier checks the measurement part of fault-free validity by testing whether the fault-free circuit preserves the extended stabilizer group: $C\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{ext}}C^\dagger=\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{ext}}$. This condition ensures that the data-qubit stabilizer constraints and the measurement-qubit preparation constraints are restored by the fault-free circuit. Since each measurement qubit is measured in its preparation basis, the measurement outcomes are deterministic and have the expected fault-free values.

The verifier then checks the logical-identity part of fault-free validity. Let $\overline{X}_1,\ldots,\overline{X}_k,\overline{Z}_1,\ldots,\overline{Z}_k$ be the chosen logical Pauli generators on the data block. For each such generator $L$, let $L^{\mathrm{ext}}=L\otimes I_{\mathrm{anc}}$ be its extension to the full data-and-measurement-qubit system. The verifier checks whether $(CL^{\mathrm{ext}}C^\dagger)\cdot L^{\mathrm{ext}}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{ext}}$. If this condition holds for every logical generator, then the induced operation on the encoded data is logically equivalent to the identity.

The circuit passes Step~1 exactly when both the extended-stabilizer preservation check and the logical-generator check succeed.


\subsection{Overall verification algorithm}
\begin{algorithm}[t]
\DontPrintSemicolon
\SetAlgoLined
\caption{SAT-Based Verification of a Flagged Syndrome-Extraction Protocol}
\label{alg:verification}
\KwIn{Stabilizer code $\mathcal Q$, flagged protocol $\Pi$, fault bound $t$}
\KwOut{\textbf{True} if $\Pi$ passes verification; \textbf{False} otherwise}

\ForEach{syndrome-extraction circuit $C$ in $\Pi$}{
    \lIf{Step~1 fails for $C$}{\Return \textbf{False}}
    \lIf{Step~2 fails for $C$}{\Return \textbf{False}}
}

\ForEach{flagged syndrome-extraction circuit $C$ in $\Pi$}{
    \For{$w \gets 1$ \KwTo $t$}{
        \lIf{Step~3 fails for $C$ and $w$}{\Return \textbf{False}}
    }
}

\ForEach{terminating path $\pi$ of $\Pi$}{
    \lIf{Step~4 fails for $\pi$}{\Return \textbf{False}}
}

\Return \textbf{True}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:verification} summarizes the complete verification procedure. Given a stabilizer code, a flagged syndrome-extraction protocol, and a fault bound \(t\), the verifier first performs the circuit-level checks from Steps~1--3. Step~1 checks fault-free validity using deterministic stabilizer simulation. Step~2 checks syndrome correctness by searching for an input Pauli error that causes an incorrect syndrome bit. Step~3 checks the flag condition by searching, for each \(w=1,\ldots,t\), for an unflagged \(w\)-fault execution whose residual error is not stabilizer-equivalent to any Pauli error of weight at most \(w\).

If all circuit-level checks succeed, the verifier enumerates the terminating paths of the protocol and performs the Step~4 bad-pair query on each path. This query searches for two fault assignments that follow the same terminating path, produce the same measured generalized syndrome, and leave residual data errors that are not stabilizer-equivalent.

The verifier rejects as soon as any check produces a counterexample. If all checks are unsatisfiable, the protocol is accepted.

\begin{theorem}[Verification correctness]
\label{thm:verification-correctness}
Let \(\Pi\) be a flagged syndrome-extraction protocol for a stabilizer code \(\mathcal Q\), and let \(t\) be the fault bound. If Algorithm~\ref{alg:verification} returns \textbf{True}, then \(\Pi\) satisfies the assumptions of Theorem~\ref{thm:fault-tolerant-recovery}. Consequently, there exists a lookup-table decoder such that the resulting error-correction procedure is \(t\)-fault tolerant.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If Algorithm~\ref{alg:verification} returns \textbf{True}, then every syndrome-extraction circuit passes Step~1 and is fault-free valid. Every syndrome-extraction circuit also passes Step~2 and therefore reports the correct stabilizer syndrome in the fault-free case for arbitrary input Pauli errors. Furthermore, every flagged syndrome-extraction circuit passes Step~3 and therefore satisfies the flag condition.

Finally, every terminating path passes the Step~4 bad-pair query. By the construction of the query, this implies that any two executions producing the same measured generalized syndrome leave stabilizer-equivalent residual data errors. Hence the decoder-existence condition of Section~\ref{sec:flagged-decoder-existence} holds.

Theorem~\ref{thm:fault-tolerant-recovery} therefore applies and guarantees the existence of a lookup-table decoder that makes the protocol \(t\)-fault tolerant.
\end{proof}

\section{Related Work}

Recent works have made significant progress in verifying quantum error correction circuits using SAT solvers. For example, Chen et al.~\cite{chen2025verifying} employ symbolic execution to check if injected faults in quantum gadgets violate fault-tolerance conditions. Similarly, Huang et al.~\cite{10.1145/3729293} introduce Veri-QEC, a formal verification framework that translates Pauli errors into classical assertions solved by SAT solvers. 

However, applying these general methods to flagged syndrome extraction circuits creates a major bottleneck if an explicit decoder is not provided. In these frameworks, two distinct faults might produce the exact same syndrome. Without a predefined decoder to resolve this ambiguity, their tools cannot determine whether the post-recovery state will actually cause a logical error. Furthermore, their assertions struggle when an error propagates to a weight strictly greater than $t$---a scenario that naturally occurs in flagged protocols.

Our work bypasses these limitations by adopting a specialized approach. By focusing strictly on flagged syndrome extraction protocols, we completely eliminate the need for a decoder during the verification process. Instead of directly evaluating standard fault-tolerance conditions, we verify a necessary and sufficient property: \emph{distinguishability}. If a flagged protocol passes our distinguishability check, we can mathematically guarantee the existence of a valid look-up-table (LUT) decoder for it. Beyond these theoretical guarantees, our approach demonstrates clear practical utility; by applying our method, we successfully identified previously unknown design flaws in published flagged syndrome extraction protocols.

Our experiments were conducted on a MacBook Pro with an M4 Pro chip and 48GB of RAM. We implemented our verification framework using Z3py to encode the constraints, which were subsequently transformed into DIMACS format for the CryptoMiniSat solver. CryptoMiniSat was chosen for its highly optimized performance on formulas containing massive XOR clauses; all tasks were run without a timeout. 

An interesting finding in Table~\ref{tab:experiment-results} is that the $[[19,1,5]]$ protocols from \cite{Liou25} and \cite{Du24} failed Step 2 but passed Step 3. This means that while some individual circuits fail to raise a flag for high-weight errors, the overall protocol remains safe. As noted in \cite{Liou25, Du24}, a local failure in a single flagged extraction circuit does not necessarily break the distinguishability of the entire protocol. Our results formally confirm this: the multi-round structure of these protocols ensures that even if a flag is missed in one round, the final syndrome remains unique and the errors can still be distinguished.


By applying our tool to existing flagged syndrome extraction protocols, we identified several design flaws that violate the stated fault-tolerance properties. We highlight two representative flaws discovered by our verification, both of which were found in protocols based on the $[[5,1,3]]$ stabilizer code. 

\paragraph{Example 1 (\cite{Liou25} protocol with the $[2,2]^T$ circuit).}
Fig.~\ref{fig:[[5,1,3]]-[2,2]^T-fail} shows a counterexample for the $[[5,1,3]]$
code using the $[2,2]^T$ syndrome-extraction circuit together with the Liou
($d=3$) protocol. The two subfigures correspond to the first round of the
protocol under two different single-gate faults. In both cases, the fault (red)
induces a Pauli error that propagates through the circuit (blue). Although the
resulting Pauli operators on the ancilla and flag qubits differ, the measurement
outcomes are identical: since the ancilla is prepared and measured in the $X$
basis, both $Y$ and $Z$ errors have the same effect on the measurement result.

Because the fault occurs in the first round, the second (last) round (not shown) is
fault-free. The two resulting data-qubit errors, $IIZIX$ and $IZIIZ$, produce the
same syndrome in the last round. Therefore, the entire generalized
syndrome (including syndrome and flag outcomes across rounds) is identical in
the two executions. However, the two data-qubit errors are non-degenerate, which
violates Proposition~\ref{prop:flagged_syndrome_extraction_protocol}.

Using our verification framework, we can rapidly evaluate new circuit designs. \paragraph{Example 2 (Low-depth adaptive protocol \cite{low-depth}).}
We also found a counterexample in the low-depth adaptive protocol of
\cite{low-depth}. Fig.~\ref{fig:[[5,1,3]]-low-depth-fault} shows two executions in
which a single fault occurs in the first-round measurement of $XZZXI$ using a
flag qubit. In one case, the fault on the first $\mathrm{CZ}$ gate induces an
$XY$ error; in the other case, a fault on the second $\mathrm{CZ}$ gate induces a
$YY$ error. In both executions, the syndrome bit and the flag outcome are
identical. The protocol then proceeds to measure $XZZXI$, $YXXYI$, and $XIXZZ$
without flags, yielding identical measurement outcomes across the two runs.
Nevertheless, the resulting data-qubit errors are $IIYXI$ and $IXZXI$, which are
non-degenerate. Hence, the protocol violates
Proposition~\ref{prop:flagged_syndrome_extraction_protocol}.

Designing flagged syndrome extraction circuits is highly error-prone; even for small codes such as the $[[5,1,3]]$ code, pursuing different optimization goals can introduce faults that are difficult to identify manually. To address this, our verification framework enables rapid prototyping and iteration, substantially reducing the time required to develop and validate new extraction protocols for diverse quantum codes.

\section{Conclusion}
In this paper, we presented a formal verification framework for fault-tolerant flagged syndrome extraction protocols. Instead of directly verifying standard fault-tolerance definitions, we verified a necessary and sufficient distinguishability condition. While many protocols include manual proofs of fault tolerance, advanced optimizations---such as reduced circuit depth or flag qubit sharing---complicate gate ordering and error propagation, making manual verification prone to error. Our automated, SAT-based approach accelerates the design process by rapidly identifying whether a proposed circuit or protocol contains design flaws. 

In future work, we plan to extend our Pauli-tracking encoding to verify other fault-tolerant gadgets and broader quantum computing architectures, such as measurement-based quantum computation.

\section{Lemma Proof}
\begin{lemma}
For a QECC with a stabilizer group $\mathcal{S} = \langle g_1, \dots, g_{n-k} \rangle$ and logical operators $\mathcal{L} = \{\bar{X}_1, \dots, \bar{X}_k, \bar{Z}_1, \dots, \bar{Z}_k\}$, if a Pauli string $P$ commutes with all generators $g_i$ and all logical operators $\bar{X}_j, \bar{Z}_j$, then $P$ lies in the group $\mathcal{S}$ (up to an overall phase).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The codespace $\mathcal{C}$ stabilized by the group $\mathcal{S}$ is defined as:
\[
\mathcal{C} = \{\,|\psi\rangle : G|\psi\rangle = |\psi\rangle \ \forall G \in \mathcal{S}\,\}.
\]
Since $P$ commutes with every generator $G_i$, it commutes with all of $\mathcal{S}$. Hence, for any
$|\psi\rangle \in \mathcal{C}$ and any $G \in \mathcal{S}$,
\[
G(P|\psi\rangle) = P(G|\psi\rangle) = P|\psi\rangle,
\]
so $P$ maps $\mathcal{C}$ to itself.

Fix the logical computational basis $\{|z\rangle_L\}_{z\in\{0,1\}^k} \subset \mathcal{C}$
defined by
\[
\bar{Z}_j |z\rangle_L = (-1)^{z_j}|z\rangle_L \qquad (j=1,\dots,k).
\]
Because $P$ commutes with every $\bar{Z}_j$, it preserves each joint eigenspace of the
commuting family $\{\bar{Z}_j\}_{j=1}^k$. In particular, each $|z\rangle_L$ is an eigenvector of
$P$:
\[
P|z\rangle_L = \lambda_z |z\rangle_L
\]
for some phase $\lambda_z$ (indeed $\lambda_z \in \{\pm 1, \pm i\}$ since $P \in \mathcal{P}_n$).

Now use commutation with the logical $\bar{X}_j$. For any $j$ and any bitstring $z$,
\[
P(\bar{X}_j|z\rangle_L) = \bar{X}_j(P|z\rangle_L) = \lambda_z\,\bar{X}_j|z\rangle_L.
\]
Since $\bar{X}_j|z\rangle_L = |z\oplus e_j\rangle_L$, the left-hand side is also
\[
P|z\oplus e_j\rangle_L = \lambda_{z\oplus e_j}|z\oplus e_j\rangle_L,
\]
hence
\[
\lambda_{z\oplus e_j} = \lambda_z \qquad \forall z,\ \forall j.
\]
Because the bit flips $z \mapsto z\oplus e_j$ connect all bitstrings in $\{0,1\}^k$, it follows that
$\lambda_z$ is independent of $z$; write $\lambda_z=\lambda$ for all $z$. Therefore,
\[
P|\psi\rangle = \lambda |\psi\rangle \qquad \forall |\psi\rangle \in \mathcal{C},
\]
i.e., $P$ acts as a global phase on the entire codespace.

Equivalently, the induced logical operator of $P$ is trivial. Hence $P$ represents the
identity element in the quotient $C(\mathcal{S})/\mathcal{S}$, where $C(\mathcal{S})$ is the centralizer of $\mathcal{S}$. Thus, there exists an element $S' \in \mathcal{S}$ such that
$P = \lambda S'$. We conclude that $P \in \mathcal{S}$ up to an overall phase, as claimed.
\end{proof}
